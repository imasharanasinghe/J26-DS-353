## ABSTRACT

Artificial-intelligence-assisted learning can provide flexible and personalized support, but current educational systems still face a trade-off between structured pedagogical control and fluent natural-language generation. Prior research on adaptive learning shows benefits from personalization, while studies of LLM-based tutoring also report reliability problems such as incorrect, unsupported, or overly general feedback. For undergraduate Data Structures and Algorithms learning, these issues are important because explanations must not only be factually correct but also match prerequisite knowledge and the learner's current cognitive level.

This proposal investigates Bloom-Level-Aware Faithfulness for Personalized LLM Explanations, the fourth component of a larger framework for verified personalized learning-path generation. The identified research gap is that factual faithfulness and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness are typically treated separately; existing approaches do not clearly provide claim-level verification that treats a factually correct but cognitively inappropriate explanation as a faithfulness violation.

The proposed component will generate path-oriented explanations using GPT-4o, decompose them into atomic claims, verify factual entailment against a pedagogy-typed knowledge graph, and verify cognitive appropriateness using Bloom-related constraints and learner mastery information. Detected violations will be classified, selectively rewritten, and re-verified, producing a personalized explanation with knowledge-graph grounding and a faithfulness report.

Following feedback from the proposal panel, the component also treats cognitive appropriateness as a trajectory rather than a snapshot. It verifies that each explanation targets the learner's next Bloom level (one level above the level the learner has demonstrated, when the prerequisites are mastered), treats an explanation that offers no task at that level as a stagnation violation, fades scaffolding as the learner keeps succeeding, and updates the learner's demonstrated Bloom level from check questions. The study asks whether this raises demonstrated cognitive level across sessions compared with matched-level and unverified explanation.

Design Science Research will guide the study, supported by iterative prototyping and comparative experimental evaluation. The proposed system will be compared with an unverified GPT-4o baseline and factual-only/cognitive-only ablations. Evaluation will use hallucination rate, cognitive-mismatch rate, verifier precision, recall and F1, rewrite success, a multi-session comparison of demonstrated Bloom level (first with simulated learners, then in a small pilot), and a small undergraduate user study of clarity and comprehension. Component 4 consumes the knowledge graph from Component 1, learner state from Component 2, and verified path from Component 3, forming the final explanation and verification layer of the integrated middleware.

**Keywords:** Large Language Models, Personalized Learning, Faithfulness Verification, Bloom's Taxonomy, Knowledge Graphs

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Background and Context

Generative AI and Large Language Models are changing how university students access educational support. This raises the importance of ensuring that AI-generated educational guidance is accurate, pedagogically appropriate, and aligned with learners' actual knowledge states.

Personalized and adaptive learning offers one way to address differences in learners' prior knowledge and progress. A 2024 scoping review of 69 studies reported improved academic performance in 41 studies (59%) and increased engagement in 25 studies (36%) [1]. The same review shows that adaptive learning is technologically and pedagogically complex. Effective personalization requires more than generating different content for different learners. The learning sequence must account for prerequisite knowledge, current mastery, and the suitability of material to the learner's state.

These requirements matter particularly in Data Structures and Algorithms, the educational domain selected for this research. DSA concepts depend heavily on prerequisite knowledge. Understanding balanced search trees may require prior understanding of basic tree structures, binary search trees, and recursion. Empirical work in data-structures education shows that weaknesses in prerequisite skills and misconceptions from earlier topics contribute to difficulties with more advanced concepts [2]. Students can therefore arrive at the same advanced topic with substantially different conceptual weaknesses.

Existing educational technologies address parts of this problem. Rule-based and ontology-based adaptive systems can enforce prerequisites and control the order of delivery, but they are less flexible at generating natural-language explanations that adapt to individual learner states. LLM-based tutors generate fluent explanations and respond flexibly to queries, but their generated reasoning is not inherently guaranteed to satisfy pedagogical constraints. A trade-off exists between structured but rigid adaptive systems and flexible tutoring systems with weaker guarantees.

Commercial systems show what LLM-assisted tutoring can already do. Khanmigo guides learners through questions, explanations, and hints instead of simply supplying answers [3]. Khan Academy acknowledges that the underlying technology may produce mistakes and hallucinations, and advises users to verify AI-generated content [4]. Coursera reports that Coach has supported more than one million learners, with a 9.5% higher first-attempt quiz pass rate and 11.6% more lessons completed per hour [5]. These are vendor-reported figures, not independent evaluations.

Reliability remains the central concern. Generated text can appear convincing while containing incorrect information, and such errors are hardest to detect for learners who lack the prior knowledge to spot them. Fluency alone is not evidence that an educational explanation is trustworthy. An explanation produced for a learner should be grounded in verified domain knowledge instead of depending exclusively on the probabilistic generation of an LLM.

Component 4 therefore introduces cognitive appropriateness as a first-class faithfulness criterion alongside factual entailment. In this formulation, a factually correct claim that exceeds the learner's current Bloom-level ceiling or depends on concepts not yet mastered is treated as a faithfulness violation, not a stylistic problem. This is the central individual contribution of the proposal.

The wider research develops a knowledge-graph-grounded middleware framework of four components. Component 1 constructs a pedagogy-typed knowledge graph of DSA concepts and pedagogical relationships. Component 2 models the learner's concept-level mastery and uncertainty. Component 3 generates and verifies a personalized, prerequisite-respecting learning path. Component 4, the focus of this proposal, consumes the verified path, the graph, and the learner state to generate an explanation and verify it for both factual entailment and cognitive appropriateness. Violating claims are selectively corrected before delivery. The output is a verified personalized explanation, inline knowledge-graph citations, and a faithfulness report.

The research sits within the Centre of Excellence for AI research cluster and the EdTech and e-learning application domain. It contributes to UN Sustainable Development Goal 4, and specifically to Target 4.4 on technical and vocational skills, by improving the trustworthiness and learner appropriateness of AI-assisted educational explanations.

### 1.2 Literature Review

#### 1.2.1 Personalized and Adaptive Learning

Adaptive learning systems modify content, sequencing, feedback, or activities according to differences among learners. The 2024 scoping review by du Plooy, Casteleijn and Franzsen examined 69 studies of personalized adaptive learning in higher education published between 2012 and 2024. Improved academic performance was reported in 41 studies and increased engagement in 25. Pre-knowledge assessments were among the most common signals for activating adaptive delivery. The review also identified technological and implementation limitations, indicating that personalization alone does not guarantee an effective system [1].

This limitation is directly relevant here. An adaptive system may know that two learners have different knowledge states and provide different feedback, but that does not demonstrate that the content of each generated explanation is cognitively appropriate for its recipient. Adaptation and verification are different problems. A system can personalize an explanation without checking whether every claim assumes knowledge the learner has actually mastered.

Cognitive-load research supports the same point. Skulmowski and Xu show that the demands imposed by identical instructional material differ with prior knowledge. A novice may experience greater intrinsic load from the same task than an expert, and information that helps a low-expertise learner may become redundant or detrimental for a more experienced one [6]. Digital-learning design should account for learner expertise instead of assuming one presentation suits all users.

#### 1.2.2 LLMs and Intelligent Tutoring Systems

LLMs have extended Intelligent Tutoring Systems by enabling natural-language interaction and scalable feedback generation. Reddig, Arora and MacLellan investigated whether GPT-4 could diagnose student errors and generate targeted corrective feedback within the Apprentice Tutor College Algebra ITS. GPT-4 identified a variety of learner errors and produced relevant, specific feedback in many cases, showing that LLM generation can be integrated with information about an actual learner interaction [7].

The same study provides the clearest evidence for the present research problem. Thirty-five percent of generated hints were judged too general, incorrect, or revealing of the answer, despite the model receiving learner and tutoring context. Only 35% of feedback passed their automated helpfulness evaluations. Supplying an LLM with learner information improves contextual relevance but does not guarantee that the resulting response is appropriate or reliable. This motivates an explicit verification stage between generation and delivery.

More recent systems reduce reliability problems through grounding. Bai, Lui and Khiatani evaluated GPTutor, a GenAI-powered ITS deployed in an undergraduate AI and Data Analytics course [8]. GPTutor uses Retrieval-Augmented Generation over an instructor-endorsed course knowledge base and can incorporate a learner profile and intended learning outcomes. Using survey and interview data from 880 responses, of which 727 were retained, the study found that use of the knowledge-grounded question-answering feature was positively associated with behavioural and emotional engagement, though not cognitive engagement.

GPTutor matters here because it shows that grounded, learner-aware GenAI tutoring already exists. The contribution of this research cannot be framed as grounding an LLM in educational content or providing personalized responses. The GPTutor evaluation was conducted in a single introductory course without an experimental comparison group, and it examined student engagement rather than whether individual generated claims satisfied explicit factual and cognitive constraints [8]. Grounded and learner-aware generation therefore already exists. What remains unestablished is whether each generated statement satisfies explicit factual and cognitive constraints before delivery.

#### 1.2.3 Factual Faithfulness and Hallucination Detection

Fluent generation does not imply factual correctness, and recent work has investigated how to evaluate whether individual claims in generated text are supported by reliable evidence. For Component 4, factual faithfulness means whether claims in an LLM-generated explanation can be supported by the verified domain knowledge available to the system.

FActScore decomposes generated text into atomic facts and calculates the percentage supported by a designated knowledge source, instead of assigning one binary label to an entire response [9]. Min et al. used their automated method to evaluate 6,500 generations from 13 language models, with an error below 2% against human judgements. Their analysis shows why fine-grained verification is necessary: a single passage can contain both supported and unsupported information. The principle of decomposing explanations into smaller verifiable claims applies directly to Component 4. However, FActScore was designed as a general factuality metric and evaluated largely on biographical content. It does not model educational prerequisites, learner mastery, or Bloom-level requirements. A claim could receive full factual support under FActScore while being unsuitable for the learner receiving it.

SelfCheckGPT takes a different approach, exploiting consistency among multiple stochastic responses from the same model instead of checking against an external source [10]. Information the model reliably knows should stay consistent across samples, whereas hallucinated content is more likely to vary. The method improved sentence-level hallucination detection without requiring model probabilities or an external database. Its black-box nature is a practical strength when only an API is available. Self-consistency and truth are not equivalent, though: multiple generations may repeat the same unsupported belief. More importantly, SelfCheckGPT evaluates consistency in relation to factuality, not whether an educational statement conforms to a verified pedagogical representation or to an individual learner state. Component 4 needs an external source of authority, which is the pedagogy-typed knowledge graph produced by Component 1.

Natural Language Inference offers a further mechanism. Laban et al. revisited NLI-based factual-consistency detection through SummaC and identified a granularity problem: applying sentence-level NLI models to long documents substantially affects accuracy [11]. Their SummaCConv method divides documents into sentence units and aggregates entailment across pairs, achieving 74.4% balanced accuracy on a six-dataset benchmark, roughly five percentage points above the compared prior approach. Two findings transfer to Component 4. NLI is a feasible entailment mechanism for checking whether generated statements are supported, and verification granularity matters. Comparing a whole explanation against a large body of knowledge can conceal individual unsupported statements, whereas claim-level comparison identifies violations precisely.

These methods define a faithful statement in terms of whether it is true, supported, consistent, or entailed. That definition is insufficient for the educational setting studied here. An explanation of an AVL-tree operation might be fully supported by the DSA knowledge graph while assuming concepts the learner has not yet mastered. From a conventional factuality perspective it is faithful. From the perspective proposed here it is not faithful to the learner. This research extends explanation faithfulness from factual support alone to the conjunction of factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness.

#### 1.2.4 Cognitive Appropriateness and Bloom-Aware Adaptation

Instructional material may be entirely correct while requiring concepts or reasoning that exceed the learner's preparation. Cognitive appropriateness concerns whether an explanation is compatible with the learner's current mastery state and with the level of cognitive processing expected at that stage.

The revised taxonomy described by Krathwohl distinguishes six cognitive-process categories, from Remember through Create [12]. It was developed to classify educational objectives and improve alignment among objectives, instruction and assessment, so it provides a structured vocabulary for representing expected cognitive activity. It was not designed as a direct measurement of an individual learner's ability. Using Bloom levels to personalize LLM explanations therefore requires an explicit mechanism connecting the cognitive demand of content to evidence about the learner's present knowledge.

In this research, the Bloom ceiling denotes the highest Bloom cognitive-process level at which explanatory content may be pitched for a given learner at a given point in the learning path. It is an operational construct defined by the proposed system, derived from the learner's mastery state and the Bloom-related properties in the knowledge graph. It is not an established educational standard, and its computation and thresholds are defined and experimentally justified within this research rather than adopted from prior work.

Almatrafi and Johri provide evidence that Bloom-level classification can be automated. Their 2025 study tested GPT-4 on classifying 1,000 annotated course learning outcomes against the revised taxonomy. Context-rich prompting achieved the strongest GPT-4 performance, with agreement ranging from moderate to substantial, but a fine-tuned BERT classifier outperformed the best prompt-based approach [13]. Two conclusions follow for Component 4. Automated estimation of Bloom level is feasible, and such classification should not be assumed perfectly reliable. The verifier should therefore use an explicitly evaluated Bloom-classification mechanism instead of asking an LLM to judge whether its own explanation is simple enough. Almatrafi and Johri classify course learning outcomes, not learner-specific generated explanations, and do not combine cognitive-level classification with a learner mastery model or factual verification.

The distinction between content-level cognitive demand and learner-level preparedness is essential. Two students may be expected to reach the same DSA learning outcome from different intermediate states. An explanation analysing AVL-tree balancing may suit a learner who has mastered binary search trees and tree rotations, while assuming unsupported prerequisite knowledge for another. The educational objective is not lowered for the second learner. The explanation presented at the current stage should use concepts and reasoning compatible with that learner's existing state until further mastery is established.

Bloom-aware adaptation is therefore not itself novel. Educational systems have used Bloom levels for recommendation and sequencing, and LLMs have been used both to generate content at specified levels and to classify content by cognitive level. The unresolved issue is narrower. These approaches do not implement a post-generation mechanism in which an explanation is checked simultaneously for knowledge-grounded factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness. Factual entailment asks whether a claim is supported. Cognitive appropriateness asks whether a supported claim is suitable for this learner at this stage. A claim must satisfy both before it is considered faithful for delivery.

#### 1.2.5 Knowledge-Graph Grounding in Educational AI

Knowledge graphs represent domain knowledge as concepts and explicit relationships among them. In education they have supported personalized learning, curriculum organization, recommendation, semantic search, and intelligent tutoring. They are most useful where educational decisions depend not just on individual concepts but on the relationships between them.

Agrawal et al. surveyed approaches combining knowledge graphs with LLMs to reduce hallucination, categorizing them by the stage at which external structured knowledge is introduced [14]. Their review reports that graphs improve factual grounding and reasoning, but emphasizes that graph augmentation is not a complete solution and that effectiveness depends on graph quality, retrieval, integration method, and evaluation strategy. This caution matters for Component 4. The system should not assume that a response becomes faithful because graph information appeared in its prompt. The graph is intended to function as an external verification substrate. Generated claims are mapped to relevant graph evidence and checked after generation. This preserves the distinction between grounded generation, which supplies trusted information to the model, and verified generation, which evaluates whether the model's actual output remains consistent with that information.

Knowledge graphs do not make document-based retrieval unnecessary, and the literature does not establish that graph retrieval is universally superior. Their relevance here follows from the type of information Component 4 must verify. Concept relationships, prerequisites, Bloom-related properties, and learner-dependent constraints are naturally represented as structured entities and edges instead of being inferred repeatedly from retrieved prose. If an explanation makes a claim about the relationship between binary search trees and AVL trees, the factual verifier retrieves the relevant concepts and relations from the DSA graph and determines whether the claim is supported. The same structure then contributes to a separate cognitive check by exposing prerequisite and Bloom-related information, while the learner's mastery state from Component 2 determines whether those concepts are currently appropriate. The graph therefore plays two distinct roles: evidence for factual entailment, and pedagogical metadata for cognitive verification.

Component 4 does not claim novelty from combining an LLM with a knowledge graph, but from using the graph as an explicit source of evidence within a dual verification process applied before delivery.

#### 1.2.6 Existing Commercial Systems

Khanmigo and Coursera Coach demonstrate that personalized conversational support can be deployed at scale, and both provide practical benchmarks. Their internal architectures and evaluation procedures are proprietary, so conclusions about their verification mechanisms are limited to publicly available information.

Khanmigo is an AI tutor integrated into the Khan Academy platform [3]. Khan Academy states that its updated tutoring experience gives Khanmigo information about a student's mastery of a skill and its prerequisite skills, adapts support according to whether the learner is new to a skill or reviewing it, and may recommend prerequisite review. Commercial AI tutoring has therefore progressed beyond simple prompt-based personalization, and this research cannot claim novelty merely from incorporating learner mastery or prerequisite awareness. Khan Academy also acknowledges that Khanmigo may produce errors or hallucinations and recommends that output be verified [4]. Learner-aware generation alone does not guarantee reliable content.

Coursera Coach provides interactive guidance, explanations, and real-time feedback grounded in Coursera course content, with instructor-defined learning objectives and assessment criteria [5]. Coursera reports over one million learners supported. Coach shows why the gap should not be described as a lack of grounding. Grounding the generation process does not demonstrate that each statement in the resulting explanation has been verified for entailment, nor that every factually valid statement is cognitively suitable for its recipient.

### 1.3 Research Gap

These capabilities remain largely separated. Existing factual-faithfulness methods determine whether generated information is supported, consistent, or entailed, but not whether a factually valid claim is cognitively suitable for the learner receiving it. Bloom-aware and adaptive-learning approaches can represent cognitive demand or adapt activities to learner characteristics, but they do not treat a mismatch between generated content and the learner's cognitive state as an explicit faithfulness violation.

An LLM-generated explanation may contain no factual hallucinations yet still depend on concepts the learner has not mastered, or require analysis beyond the learner's present capability. Such an explanation passes a conventional factual-faithfulness check despite being pedagogically inappropriate. Including learner information or Bloom-level instructions in the generation prompt does not guarantee that the final explanation satisfies those constraints, because personalization during generation and verification after generation are separate problems.

This research does not claim that Bloom's Taxonomy, knowledge graphs, LLM personalization, or factuality checking are independently novel. The contribution lies in operationalizing cognitive appropriateness as part of explanation faithfulness and combining it with knowledge-grounded factual entailment in a post-generation verification process. A statement that is factually supported but exceeds the learner's permitted cognitive level, or depends on unmastered concepts, is treated as a violation requiring correction.

#### Final Research Gap Statement

Existing LLM faithfulness approaches primarily evaluate whether generated claims are factually supported, while learner-adaptive and Bloom-aware approaches address cognitive demand separately. The literature reviewed for this proposal does not establish a claim-level verification framework that treats a factually correct but learner-inappropriate explanation as a faithfulness violation by jointly considering knowledge-graph entailment, learner mastery, and Bloom-level constraints.

#### Research Questions

**RQ1:** Can the claims in an LLM-generated educational explanation be verified at atomic-claim level against a pedagogy-typed knowledge graph with sufficient precision and recall to identify unsupported and contradicted content reliably?

**RQ2:** Can learner-relative cognitive appropriateness, defined as compliance with a mastery-derived Bloom constraint and dependence only on sufficiently mastered prerequisite concepts, be operationalized as a machine-checkable faithfulness criterion, and does verifying it detect violations that factual verification alone does not?

**RQ3:** Does applying targeted correction to claims that fail either criterion reduce hallucination rate and cognitive-mismatch rate relative to an unverified LLM baseline without degrading explanation clarity or learner comprehension?

RQ1 and RQ2 concern the verification mechanism itself. RQ3 concerns whether the mechanism improves the explanation delivered to the learner. Each is addressed by a corresponding element of the evaluation design in Chapter 3.

#### Extension after the proposal-panel feedback: cognitive appropriateness over time

The panel observed that, as first proposed, the component checks cognitive appropriateness at a single moment and could therefore be read as a product that filters explanations rather than a contribution to how learners advance. The gap is real: matching an explanation to the learner's current Bloom level keeps the learner where they are. An explanation that is factually correct and never above the learner's level, yet never asks the learner to do more than they already can, supports no progress. The classic work on the zone of proximal development [18] and on tutoring as scaffolding [19] suggests the opposite design target: support at the edge of what the learner can do alone, withdrawn as competence grows.

The component therefore adds a second, time-dependent notion of appropriateness. An explanation is cognitively appropriate when it targets the learner's *next* Bloom level, on concepts whose prerequisites are mastered. Two violations follow: content above the target is a `BLOOM_MISMATCH`, and an explanation with no task at the target level is a `BLOOM_STAGNATION`. Progress is observed through short check questions whose results update a per-concept demonstrated Bloom level, which determines the next target. This turns the verifier from a filter into a mechanism that can be evaluated over a sequence of sessions.

**RQ4a:** Does level-targeted verification and repair keep explanations factually faithful while ensuring that each explanation contains a check question at the learner's target Bloom level, compared with an unverified explanation and with matched-level (static) verification?

**RQ4b:** Over several sessions, does targeting the learner's next Bloom level raise the Bloom level the learner demonstrates more than matched-level or unverified explanation, and under which assumptions about how learners learn does it fail to do so?

RQ4a is evaluated on the controlled stress corpus. RQ4b is evaluated first with simulated learners (to test the mechanism and its sensitivity, including control learner models in which progression has no advantage) and then in a small human pilot; only the pilot can support a claim about students.

### 1.4 Research Area and Key Pillars

This research sits within Data Science, at the intersection of natural language processing, knowledge representation, and applied educational measurement. The classification follows from the research question, which asks whether the faithfulness of generated natural-language text, in two distinct senses, can be measured reliably against structured evidence and a quantified model of an individual learner. Both the object of study and the evaluation are data-driven.

**Natural Language Processing:** The component operates on generated text. Atomic-claim decomposition, natural language inference for entailment checking, and automated classification of cognitive demand are all NLP tasks. The design decisions on decomposition granularity and on the choice between encoder-based entailment models and generative judgement draw on the summarization-faithfulness literature reviewed in Section 1.2.3.

**Knowledge Representation and Reasoning:** Verification is performed against a typed, directed graph in which concepts are nodes and pedagogical relations are edges. Retrieving the relevant subgraph for a claim, and reasoning over prerequisite chains to determine whether a claim depends on unmastered concepts, are knowledge-representation problems, not text-processing problems.

**Applied Educational Measurement and Learning Science:** The cognitive dimension of faithfulness derives from Bloom's revised taxonomy [12] and from cognitive-load theory [6], operationalized against concept-level mastery estimates. Defining the Bloom ceiling as a computable constraint requires translating an educational construct into a decision rule with explicit thresholds.

**Empirical Evaluation and Statistical Inference:** The contribution is a claim about measurable improvement. Establishing it requires controlled comparison against a baseline, ablation analysis to attribute the effect to the correct mechanism, manually annotated ground truth with reported inter-rater agreement, classification metrics for verifier performance, and paired statistical comparison of learner outcomes. This evaluation design is itself part of the contribution.

Component 4 is best characterized as a data-science research problem in which structured knowledge and quantified learner state are used to measure and correct a property of generated language.

## 2. OBJECTIVE

### 2.1 Main Objective

To design and evaluate a Bloom-level-aware faithfulness verification component that generates personalized explanations for verified DSA learning paths and validates them for both knowledge-graph-grounded factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness, compared with an unverified LLM baseline, and to investigate whether targeting each explanation at the learner's next Bloom level raises the cognitive level the learner demonstrates over repeated sessions.

### 2.2 Specific Objectives

- To generate personalized natural-language explanations for verified DSA learning paths using the learner state, mastered concepts, and relevant knowledge-graph context provided by Components 1–3.
- To implement claim-level factual verification that checks generated explanation claims against the relevant evidence in the pedagogy-typed knowledge graph and identifies unsupported or contradictory claims.
- To implement learner-relative cognitive verification that evaluates whether generated claims comply with the learner's Bloom-level constraint and rely only on concepts appropriate to the learner's current mastery state.
- To implement a targeted correction mechanism that rewrites or grounds only the explanation segments identified as factual or cognitive faithfulness violations, assigns a confidence tier to each verified claim, and preserves valid content.
- To evaluate the proposed component against an unverified LLM explanation baseline using hallucination rate, cognitive-mismatch rate, and a user study assessing explanation clarity and learner understanding.
- To maintain a per-concept demonstrated Bloom level from learner check-question results and to derive from it a target level for the next explanation (the level above the demonstrated level when the concept's prerequisites are mastered), with contingent scaffolding that fades as the learner succeeds and returns after a failure.
- To extend verification with target-level checking: treat content above the target as a Bloom mismatch and an explanation without a target-level check question as a stagnation violation, and repair both by targeted rewriting.
- To evaluate the Bloom-level estimator against labelled items and to compare matched-level and progressive explanation across multiple sessions, using simulated learners with control learner models and a small human pilot.

## 3. METHODOLOGY

### 3.1 Research Methodology / Research Design

This study adopts Design Science Research (DSR) as the primary research methodology, supported by comparative experimental evaluation. DSR is suitable for research in which an artifact is designed to address an identified problem and is subsequently demonstrated and evaluated. Peffers et al. describe a DSR process consisting of problem identification and motivation, definition of solution objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication [15]. This process is appropriate for the present study because the research contribution involves the development and evaluation of a computational verification component rather than only the observation of existing LLM behaviour.

The artifact developed in this research is Component 4 of the proposed personalized learning framework. Its purpose is to generate explanations for verified Data Structures and Algorithms learning paths and determine whether the generated content is faithful in two dimensions: factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness. The component receives a pedagogy-typed knowledge graph from Component 1, the learner's concept-level mastery state from Component 2, and a verified personalized learning path from Component 3. These inputs are used to generate an initial explanation, decompose it into atomic claims, verify each relevant claim, identify violations, selectively rewrite unsuitable content, and produce a final verified explanation together with supporting evidence and a faithfulness report.

The DSR process is applied to Component 4 as shown in Table 2.

| DSR Stage | Application in Component 4 |
|---|---|
| Problem identification and motivation | Establish that LLM-generated educational explanations may contain unsupported factual claims and may also be factually correct but cognitively inappropriate for a learner. |
| Define objectives of a solution | Define a verification mechanism that evaluates explanations for both factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness. |
| Design and development | Develop GPT-4o explanation generation, atomic-claim extraction, KG-based factual verification, Bloom/mastery-based cognitive verification, violation reporting, and targeted rewriting. |
| Demonstration | Apply the developed component to verified learning paths across a representative selection of DSA concepts and learner states. |
| Evaluation | Compare verified explanations with an unverified GPT-4o baseline using hallucination rate, cognitive-mismatch rate, verifier performance, and learner-based evaluation. |
| Communication | Document the research methodology, implementation, experimental results, limitations, and contribution through the dissertation, evaluation artifacts, and research presentation. |

*Table 2 - Application of the Design Science Research Process to Component 4*

The design and development process will be iterative because the proposed component consists of several dependent stages whose performance can affect subsequent stages. For example, inaccurate claim decomposition may reduce the effectiveness of factual verification, while unsuitable Bloom-level classification may affect cognitive verification. Individual modules will therefore be developed and tested before the complete pipeline is integrated.

Comparative experimental evaluation will be used within the DSR evaluation stage to determine whether the proposed artifact improves explanation faithfulness. The primary comparison will be between an explanation generated by the selected LLM without post-generation verification and an explanation generated under equivalent input conditions but processed through the complete Component 4 verification and rewriting pipeline. Using the same underlying generator in both conditions reduces the possibility that observed improvements are caused simply by differences between language models.

The experimental inputs include structured knowledge-graph evidence, learner-state information, verified learning paths, generated explanations, and manually annotated claim-level reference labels. A small learner-based evaluation will also be conducted to assess explanation clarity and immediate comprehension. The technical evaluation will measure hallucination rate, cognitive-mismatch rate, verifier precision, recall and F1-score, and rewrite success. These measures allow the study to evaluate both the quality of the final explanation and the reliability of the verification mechanism itself.

The methodology is therefore suitable for the research questions for three reasons. First, the study requires the construction of a working artifact that operationalizes the proposed dual definition of faithfulness. Second, the artifact can be evaluated objectively using claim-level reference annotations and controlled baseline comparisons. Third, the research must determine whether technical improvements in factual and cognitive verification also preserve the usefulness of the explanation for learners. DSR provides the overall framework for developing the artifact, while comparative experiments and learner-based evaluation provide the evidence required to assess its effectiveness.

### 3.2 Data / Participants / Experimental Inputs

Component 4 does not independently construct the domain knowledge graph, learner model, or personalized learning path. Instead, it consumes the verified outputs of the three preceding components. Component 1 provides the pedagogy-typed DSA knowledge graph and associated pedagogical information, Component 2 provides the learner's concept-level mastery state, and Component 3 provides the verified personalized learning path. These inputs are combined with generated explanations and manually prepared reference annotations to support the evaluation of the proposed verification mechanism.

#### 3.2.1 Knowledge-Graph Input

The first experimental input is the verified DSA knowledge graph produced by Component 1. The graph represents concepts and pedagogically relevant relationships, including prerequisite and Bloom-related information required by Component 4.

For factual verification, relevant concepts and relations associated with the current learning-path step will be retrieved from the graph and converted into a form suitable for entailment checking. For cognitive verification, prerequisite and Bloom-related information will be considered together with the learner state to determine whether a generated claim assumes knowledge or cognitive demand that is inappropriate for the learner.

Component 4 will use the graph as an external verification source and will not modify its structure or reconstruct its contents.

#### 3.2.2 Learner-State Input

The second input is the learner state produced by Component 2. This consists primarily of concept-level mastery information associated with the learner's current knowledge state.

Component 4 will use these mastery estimates to determine whether prerequisite concepts required by an explanation have been sufficiently mastered and to support the derivation of learner-relative cognitive constraints. Appropriateness will therefore be evaluated at concept level rather than by assigning a permanent label such as beginner, intermediate, or advanced.

This allows two learners working toward the same eventual learning objective to receive explanations with different levels of scaffolding according to their current mastery.

#### 3.2.3 Verified Learning-Path Input

The third upstream input is the verified personalized learning path produced by Component 3. The path identifies the sequence of concepts selected for the learner to reach a specified learning goal.

Component 4 uses this path as the context for explanation generation. The explanation may describe the current concept, its relationship to previous or upcoming concepts, and why it appears at a particular stage of the learner's sequence. Component 4 does not insert, remove, or reorder concepts in the learning path, as path generation and verification remain the responsibility of Component 3.

#### 3.2.4 Experimental DSA Coverage

Evaluation will be conducted across a representative range of Data Structures and Algorithms concepts rather than being restricted to a single topic. Candidate areas include linear data structures, stacks and queues, trees, binary search trees, balanced trees, heaps, sorting, and graph-related concepts.

The final experimental selection will include concepts with different prerequisite depths, levels of conceptual difficulty, Bloom-related cognitive demands, and dependencies on previously mastered concepts. This variation is necessary to test whether the verification mechanism remains effective under different learner and topic conditions.

#### 3.2.5 Generated Explanation Corpus

A corpus of approximately 100 to 150 LLM-generated explanations is planned for the main technical evaluation. This is a proposal-stage estimate and may be refined after pilot implementation.

Each experimental case will contain the following information:

| Experimental element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Target DSA concept/path step | Defines what is currently being explained |
| Relevant verified learning path | Provides sequencing context |
| Relevant KG evidence | Provides factual and pedagogical grounding |
| Learner mastery state | Provides learner-specific context |
| Draft GPT-4o explanation | Input to the proposed verifier |
| Atomic claims extracted from explanation | Units used for factual/cognitive verification |
| Final verified explanation | Output after violation correction |
| Faithfulness report | Records detected and corrected violations |

*Table 3 - Elements of Each Experimental Case*

The same explanation-generation model and comparable input conditions will be used when producing baseline and proposed-system samples so that the effect of the verification process can be evaluated fairly.

#### 3.2.6 Ground-Truth Annotation Data

Manual annotation will be used to create reference labels for evaluating the automatic verifier. Atomic claims from the evaluation corpus will be annotated separately for factual status and cognitive status.

Factual annotations will determine whether a claim is supported, contradicted, or insufficiently supported by the relevant knowledge-graph evidence. Cognitive annotations will determine whether the claim is appropriate under the learner's mastery state and the cognitive constraints defined for the experimental case.

A representative subset of the claims will be independently reviewed by two annotators. Disagreements will be identified and resolved before final reference labels are established. Inter-rater agreement will also be reported to provide evidence of annotation consistency.

#### 3.2.7 User-Study Participants

A small learner-based study will be conducted to evaluate explanation clarity and immediate comprehension. Approximately 20 to 30 undergraduate volunteers are planned, subject to recruitment feasibility and institutional ethical requirements.

Participants will be selected from peer or junior undergraduate populations with sufficient exposure to basic computing or DSA concepts to understand the selected explanation material. Each participant will encounter both an unverified explanation and a Component 4 verified explanation. Where practical, the presentation order will be counterbalanced to reduce order effects.

Participants will rate explanation clarity and complete short concept-related questions intended to measure immediate understanding.

Participation will be voluntary. Informed consent will be obtained before data collection, identifying information will be minimized, and results will be reported anonymously. Ethical clearance will be obtained from the relevant institutional review process before participant recruitment begins.

#### 3.2.8 Data Preparation

The experimental inputs will be prepared through the following stages:

- **Knowledge-graph preparation:** relevant concepts, relations, and supporting subgraphs will be identified for each selected DSA case.
- **Learner-state preparation:** the mastery-state representation required for each learner or experimental profile will be obtained or constructed.
- **Path preparation:** verified learning paths relevant to the selected learning goals will be obtained from Component 3.
- **Explanation generation:** initial explanations will be generated under controlled input conditions.
- **Claim decomposition:** generated explanations will be divided into atomic claims suitable for verification.
- **Annotation preparation:** factual and cognitive reference labels will be prepared for the selected claims.
- **User-study preparation:** a manageable subset of explanation pairs and corresponding comprehension questions will be selected for human evaluation.

This preparation process ensures that the proposed component is evaluated using consistent and reproducible experimental inputs rather than isolated example prompts.

### 3.3 Baseline / Benchmark

The primary baseline for evaluating Component 4 will be an unverified GPT-4o explanation. Under this condition, GPT-4o will receive the same learner context, verified learning-path information, and relevant instructional context used during explanation generation in the proposed system, but the generated explanation will be delivered without the post-generation factual-entailment and cognitive-appropriateness verification stages.

This baseline is appropriate because the research contribution of Component 4 is not the use of GPT-4o itself, but the verification mechanism added after explanation generation. Using the same underlying language model in both conditions therefore allows the experiment to isolate the contribution of the proposed verification pipeline. If different LLMs were used for the baseline and proposed system, any improvement could instead result from differences in model capability.

The comparison will therefore consist of two principal conditions:

| Condition | Description |
|---|---|
| Baseline: Unverified GPT-4o | Generates a personalized explanation using the available path and learner context, with no post-generation factual or cognitive verification. |
| Proposed Component 4 | Generates the explanation using GPT-4o, decomposes it into atomic claims, verifies factual entailment and cognitive appropriateness, identifies violations, and selectively rewrites offending content before delivery. |

*Table 4 - Primary Experimental Conditions*

The primary comparison will measure whether Component 4 produces lower hallucination rate and cognitive-mismatch rate than the unverified baseline.

In addition to the primary baseline, the evaluation includes factual-only and cognitive-only ablation conditions, which allow the contribution of each verification dimension to be measured separately. These are planned secondary benchmarks; the unverified GPT-4o condition remains the mandatory primary baseline. The ablation design is specified in Section 3.5.3.

#### Generalization across generator models

The primary experiments use GPT-4o for both the baseline and the proposed condition, isolating the effect of the verification layer. However, a verification mechanism that only works with a single proprietary model would be of limited value. A secondary generalization check will therefore repeat a reduced subset of the evaluation corpus using an open-weight instruction-tuned model (for example, a Llama- or Mistral-family model accessed through a hosted inference endpoint) as the explanation generator, with the Component 4 verification and rewriting stages held constant.

This condition is not intended as a comparison of model quality. Its purpose is to establish whether the proposed verification pipeline remains applicable when the generator is changed, and whether the observed reduction in hallucination and cognitive-mismatch rates is a property of the verification layer rather than of GPT-4o specifically.

This check is a secondary objective and will be conducted on a reduced corpus subject to available time and API budget.

#### Fairness of comparison

To make the experimental comparison valid, the baseline and proposed conditions should use equivalent input information wherever possible. The following factors will therefore be kept consistent:

- The same underlying GPT-4o model or model version
- The same target DSA concept
- The same learner-state profile
- The same verified learning path
- Comparable generation instructions
- The same generation parameters where technically feasible.

The major controlled difference will be the presence of the Component 4 verification and rewriting stages.

This is important because the purpose of the study is to determine whether verification improves explanation faithfulness, not whether a more detailed prompt or a newer language model outperforms a weaker baseline.

#### Relationship to existing faithfulness methods

Methods such as FActScore and SelfCheckGPT, reviewed previously, provide useful conceptual benchmarks for factuality assessment. However, they are not ideal as the primary experimental baseline because they were designed as general factuality-evaluation methods rather than as personalized educational explanation systems. Their main role in this proposal is therefore to inform the factual-verification design and to position the research contribution in relation to prior work.

The experimentally relevant comparison is instead between an ordinary personalized LLM explanation and the same explanation-generation process augmented by the proposed dual verification mechanism.

### 3.4 Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation of Component 4 will assess both the quality of the final generated explanations and the accuracy of the verification mechanism. Since the proposed contribution extends explanation faithfulness beyond factual correctness to include learner-relative cognitive appropriateness, the two principal outcome measures will be hallucination rate and cognitive-mismatch rate. Additional metrics will evaluate violation detection, rewrite effectiveness, explanation clarity, and learner understanding.

#### 3.4.1 Hallucination Rate

Hallucination rate measures the proportion of atomic factual claims in an explanation that are not supported by the relevant knowledge-graph evidence.

A claim will be considered a factual violation when it is classified as:

- Unsupported, because sufficient evidence cannot be found in the relevant graph context; or
- Contradicted, because available graph evidence conflicts with the generated claim.

The planned calculation is:

```
Hallucination Rate = (Unsupported Claims + Contradicted Claims) / Total Factual Claims × 100
```

A lower hallucination rate indicates greater factual faithfulness.

#### 3.4.2 Cognitive-Mismatch Rate

Cognitive-mismatch rate measures the proportion of explanation claims that are judged inappropriate for the learner's current state even when they may be factually valid.

A cognitive violation may occur when a claim:

- Requires a cognitive operation above the learner's permitted Bloom-level constraint;
- Depends on prerequisite concepts that the learner has not sufficiently mastered; or
- Introduces explanatory reasoning whose assumed knowledge is inconsistent with the learner state.

The planned metric is:

```
Cognitive-Mismatch Rate = Cognitively Inappropriate Claims / Total Evaluated Claims × 100
```

A lower cognitive-mismatch rate indicates greater learner-relative cognitive appropriateness.

#### 3.4.3 Verifier Precision, Recall, and F1-Score

The proposed verifier must itself be evaluated against manually annotated reference labels. Otherwise, the system would effectively be judging its own correctness.

For each violation type, the following metrics will be calculated:

In the expressions below, TP denotes true positives (claims correctly identified as violations), FP false positives (non-violating claims incorrectly flagged), and FN false negatives (actual violations the verifier failed to detect).

**Precision**

```
Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
```

This measures how often a claim flagged as a violation is actually a violation according to the reference annotation.

**Recall**

```
Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
```

This measures how many of the true violations present in the evaluation corpus are detected by the verifier.

**F1-score**

```
F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
```

This provides a balanced measure of violation-detection performance.

Where feasible, these metrics will be reported separately for:

- Factual violations;
- Bloom-level cognitive violations;
- Unmastered-prerequisite violations.

This separation will help identify which part of the verifier performs well and where improvements may be required.

#### 3.4.4 Rewrite Success Rate

Because Component 4 does not merely detect violations but also attempts to correct them, the effectiveness of the rewriting stage should be evaluated separately.

A proposed secondary metric is:

```
Rewrite Success Rate = Violating Claims Successfully Corrected / Violating Claims Sent for Rewriting × 100
```

A rewrite will be considered successful when the revised claim no longer violates the factual or cognitive constraint that triggered the correction and does not introduce a new violation.

This metric is useful because high detection accuracy alone would not demonstrate that the final learner-facing explanation is improved.

#### 3.4.5 Explanation Clarity

The planned user study will assess whether the verification process affects how understandable the explanation is to students.

Participants will rate explanation clarity using a short Likert-scale instrument, for example:

1 - Very unclear
2 - Unclear
3 - Neutral
4 - Clear
5 - Very clear

The mean or median clarity score for verified explanations will be compared with the score for unverified explanations.

The purpose is not simply to maximize linguistic simplicity. A verified explanation should remain understandable while satisfying factual and cognitive constraints.

#### 3.4.6 Learner Understanding / Comprehension Score

A short follow-up assessment will be used to determine whether participants understood the explanation they were shown. Questions will be related directly to the DSA concept or reasoning presented in the explanation.

A simple measure can be calculated as:

```
Comprehension Score = Correct Answers / Total Questions × 100
```

The verified and unverified explanation conditions will then be compared.

This metric is particularly valuable because an explanation may receive a high subjective clarity rating while still failing to produce correct understanding. Using both measures therefore provides stronger evidence than relying on satisfaction ratings alone.

#### 3.4.7 Bloom-Level Progression Metrics

Four measures are added for the progression extension (Section 1.3, RQ4a and RQ4b).

```text
Target-Level Question Rate =
  Explanations with an accepted check question at the target level /
  Total explanations × 100

Stagnation Rate =
  Explanations with no accepted check question at the target level /
  Total explanations × 100

Capability Gain (per learner) =
  Bloom capability after the final session − Bloom capability before the first session

Bloom-Estimator Accuracy =
  Items whose estimated level equals the labelled level / Total items × 100
```

Within-one-level agreement, mean absolute error, and macro-F1 are reported with accuracy because the Bloom levels are ordered. Sessions to reach a given level are reported with a censoring rule (a learner who does not reach it is counted at the study length plus one). For a simulated learner the capability is a hidden quantity that the experiment can read; for a human participant the demonstrated level is the highest level passed on the scored post-test (Section 3.5.9).

| Cognitive-Mismatch Rate | Claims inappropriate for learner mastery/Bloom constraint | Lower | C4 vs. baseline and factual-only verifier |

#### Evaluation criteria

At proposal stage, no arbitrary numerical performance threshold will be imposed before pilot testing. Instead, the principal success criteria will be based on relative improvement over the baseline.

The complete Component 4 system should demonstrate:

1. Lower hallucination rate than the unverified GPT-4o baseline;
2. Lower cognitive-mismatch rate than both the unverified baseline and, where tested, the factual-only verifier;
3. Meaningful precision, recall, and F1 performance against manually annotated violations;
4. Successful correction of a substantial proportion of detected violations without introducing new errors; and
5. Explanation clarity and learner-understanding outcomes that are at least maintained and preferably improved relative to the unverified baseline; and
6. For the progression extension: a check question at the learner's target Bloom level in every verified explanation without new violations, and, across several sessions, a demonstrated Bloom level at least as high as that under matched-level explanation, with the conditions under which progression does not help reported alongside those under which it does.

### 3.5 Validation Strategy

The proposed Component 4 will be validated through a multi-layer evaluation strategy combining manual ground-truth annotation, comparative baseline testing, ablation analysis, rewrite validation, robustness checks, and a learner-based user study. This approach is necessary because no single evaluation method can establish both the technical accuracy of the verifier and the educational usefulness of the resulting explanations.

#### 3.5.1 Manual Ground-Truth Validation

The automatic factual and cognitive verifier will not be used as the sole judge of its own performance. Instead, generated explanations will be decomposed into atomic claims and evaluated against manually assigned reference labels.

For factual verification, claims will be annotated as:

- Entailed/supported
- Contradicted or
- Unsupported.

For cognitive verification, claims will be classified according to whether they are:

- Appropriate for the defined learner state
- Above the learner-relative Bloom constraint or
- Dependent on prerequisite concepts not sufficiently mastered by the learner.

These manually assigned labels will provide the ground truth used to calculate the verifier's precision, recall, and F1-score.

Where feasible, at least two reviewers will independently annotate a representative subset of claims. Disagreements will then be discussed and resolved before establishing the final reference label. Inter-rater agreement will be reported using Cohen's kappa [17], which measures agreement between two raters while accounting for chance agreement. For this study, κ ≥ 0.70 will be used as a pre-specified operational threshold for acceptable annotation consistency. Where agreement falls below this threshold, the annotation guidelines will be revised and the affected subset re-annotated before the reference labels are used to evaluate the verifier.

#### 3.5.2 Baseline Comparison

The principal validation experiment will compare the complete Component 4 pipeline against the unverified GPT-4o baseline defined previously.

Both conditions use the equivalent inputs and generation settings specified in Section 3.3, so that the only controlled difference is whether the explanation passes through Component 4's verification and rewriting stages.

The two primary hypotheses underlying this comparison are:

**H1:** Explanations processed by Component 4 will exhibit a lower hallucination rate than unverified GPT-4o explanations.

**H2:** Explanations processed by Component 4 will exhibit a lower cognitive-mismatch rate than unverified GPT-4o explanations.

#### 3.5.3 Ablation Validation

An ablation study is planned to determine the individual contribution of the two verification mechanisms rather than evaluating only the final combined system.

The planned configurations are:

| Configuration | Factual Verification | Cognitive Verification |
|---|---|---|
| Unverified LLM | No | No |
| Factual-only | Yes | No |
| Cognitive-only | No | Yes |
| Full Component 4 | Yes | Yes |

*Table 6 - Ablation Configurations for Verification-Dimension Analysis*

This comparison is particularly important for validating the individual research novelty.

If factual-only verification reduces hallucination rate but does not substantially reduce cognitive-mismatch rate, while the complete system reduces both, this would provide evidence that cognitive appropriateness contributes an additional form of verification beyond conventional factual faithfulness.

Conversely, if cognitive verification does not improve learner-relative mismatch detection, the results would challenge the proposed contribution. The study will therefore report both positive and negative findings rather than assuming beforehand that every component will improve performance.

#### 3.5.4 Validation of the Rewriting Stage

Detected violations will be re-evaluated after targeted rewriting.

For every rewritten claim, the system will determine whether:

1. The original violation has been corrected
2. The rewritten content remains factually supported
3. The rewritten content satisfies the learner-specific cognitive constraint and
4. The rewrite has introduced any new violation.

This allows the rewrite success rate to be calculated.

Where a rewritten claim still violates a constraint, it may enter another bounded verification-rewrite iteration rather than being accepted automatically. The exact maximum number of iterations will be finalized during implementation and pilot testing.

#### 3.5.5 Robustness Across DSA Concepts and Learner States

The proposed verifier will not be evaluated using only a single DSA topic or one learner profile. The experimental corpus will include a representative selection of concepts with different prerequisite depths and cognitive demands.

Controlled learner-state profiles may also be used during development so that specific situations can deliberately be tested, for example:

- Learner has mastered all prerequisites
- Learner is missing one important prerequisite
- Learner has low mastery of several preceding concepts
- Learner is ready for higher-order reasoning
- Factual content is correct but deliberately too advanced
- Factual content contains an unsupported relationship.

These controlled cases will make it possible to determine whether the verifier reacts to known changes in learner state rather than merely producing plausible labels.

#### 3.5.6 Controlled Violation Cases

A supplementary validation technique uses deliberately injected violations.

Alongside naturally occurring LLM errors, a subset of explanations can be systematically modified to contain known violations, such as:

- An incorrect prerequisite relationship
- An unsupported DSA fact
- An explanation requiring an unmastered concept
- Reasoning deliberately set above the learner's intended Bloom constraint.

Because the location and type of these violations are known beforehand, they provide controlled test cases for measuring whether the verifier can correctly identify different violation types.

Naturally generated output cannot be guaranteed to contain sufficient instances of every violation category for reliable measurement.

The naturally generated evaluation corpus remains the principal experiment. Injected violations serve as a supplementary stress test and do not replace natural-output evaluation.

#### 3.5.7 Learner-Based Validation

Technical reductions in hallucination and cognitive mismatch do not automatically prove that an explanation is useful to students. Therefore, a small learner-based evaluation is planned.

The participant group, comparative design, and consent procedures are specified in Section 3.2.7. Two outcomes are collected:

- **Perceived clarity**, participants rate how understandable the explanation is using a Likert scale.
- **Immediate understanding**, participants answer short concept-specific comprehension questions after reading the explanation.

The user study therefore tests a different question from the automatic evaluation:

*Does reducing factual and cognitive violations also result in explanations that learners can understand effectively?*

#### 3.5.8 Statistical Analysis

The evaluation will report descriptive statistics for all major metrics, including mean, median, standard deviation where appropriate, and the observed differences between the baseline and proposed system.

Because the same or closely matched experimental cases will be evaluated under multiple conditions, paired statistical comparisons will be used where appropriate.

For continuous or approximately interval measures such as comprehension scores, the choice of statistical test will depend on the characteristics of the collected data. A paired parametric test may be used if its assumptions are reasonably satisfied; otherwise, an appropriate non-parametric alternative such as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test will be considered.

The proposal will not commit to a statistical test before examining the distribution and measurement characteristics of the collected data.

In addition to statistical significance, the magnitude and practical relevance of observed differences will be considered. A statistically detectable difference that produces little practical reduction in learner-facing violations should not automatically be interpreted as a successful educational contribution.

#### 3.5.9 Validation of Bloom-Level Progression

Three layers of evidence are used, with different strengths.

**Estimator validation.** The pilot Bloom estimator is scored against a labelled item set with a development split (used to extend the cue lexicon), a test split (scored once after the lexicon is frozen), and a split of indirectly phrased items without cue verbs that is never used for tuning. One annotator labelled the current items, so there is no inter-annotator agreement; a second annotator, a kappa of at least 0.70, and a learned or NLI-based classifier are planned.

**Simulated learners.** A seeded cohort of simulated learners studies the same concept over several sessions under paired conditions (unverified, matched-level C4, progressive C4) and two ablations (no stretch above the demonstrated level, no scaffold fading). A simulation returns its own assumptions, so three learner models are run: one in which stretching within reach teaches best, a flat control in which explanation level does not matter, and a matched control in which stretching hurts. The experiment reports where the mechanism helps, ties, and loses, the sensitivity to the number of sessions, paired differences with bootstrap intervals, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and effect sizes. Its results support the design of the policy and its limits; they are not an effect size for students.

**Human pilot.** A counterbalanced within-subject pilot with undergraduates (Section 3.2.7; protocol in the project documentation) measures the Bloom level learners demonstrate after several sessions under each condition, with two blind raters and a kappa threshold, and is explicitly exploratory. It requires ethics approval before any recruitment.

### 3.6 Development Approach

The development of Component 4 will follow an iterative prototyping approach supported by Agile principles. This approach is suitable because the proposed component consists of several interdependent modules: LLM explanation generation, atomic-claim extraction, factual verification, cognitive verification, violation reporting, targeted rewriting, and integration with the wider middleware, which are unlikely to perform optimally after a single implementation cycle.

An iterative approach allows each module to be developed, tested, refined, and integrated incrementally. This is particularly important for the proposed verifier because some implementation decisions, such as claim-decomposition quality, NLI thresholds, Bloom-related cognitive constraints, and rewrite behaviour, may require adjustment after pilot experiments. Rather than postponing testing until the entire system is complete, each stage can be validated independently before full integration.

The planned development sequence is as follows:

1. **Explanation-generation prototype** — Develop the initial GPT-4o prompting workflow that generates explanations using the verified learning path, learner state, and relevant knowledge-graph context.
2. **Atomic-claim extraction module** — Implement the mechanism that decomposes generated explanations into smaller factual and pedagogical claims suitable for independent verification.
3. **Factual-verification module** — Implement retrieval of relevant knowledge-graph evidence and the hybrid NLI/rule-based process for identifying supported, contradicted, and unsupported claims.
4. **Cognitive-verification module** — Implement the mechanism that evaluates claims against learner mastery information, prerequisite requirements, and Bloom-related cognitive constraints.
5. **Violation-reporting mechanism** — Represent detected violations using structured types so that factual and cognitive failures can be distinguished and traced to the relevant explanation segment.
6. **Targeted-rewriting mechanism** — Develop the process by which only violating claims or sentences are rewritten or grounded instead of regenerating the entire explanation.
7. **Integrated C4 prototype** — Combine the generation, verification, violation-reporting, and rewriting stages into a complete pipeline.
8. **Middleware integration** — Connect Component 4 with the shared REST API, JSON schemas, Neo4j knowledge graph, learner-state output from Component 2, and verified path output from Component 3.
9. **Pilot testing and refinement** — Evaluate the integrated prototype using selected DSA cases and controlled learner profiles, identify failure modes, and refine prompts, rules, thresholds, and verification logic.
10. **Final experimental evaluation** — Conduct the planned baseline comparisons, ablation experiments, manual validation, and learner-based evaluation.

This sequence follows the Component 4 architecture, in which outputs from Components 1, 2, and 3 are processed through LLM explanation generation, factual and cognitive verification, violation rewriting, and delivery of the verified personalized explanation.

#### Why iterative prototyping is appropriate

The selected approach is suitable for several reasons.

First, Component 4 contains both generative and deterministic/verification-oriented behaviour. The behaviour of GPT-4o cannot be assumed to remain perfectly consistent across all inputs, while the verifier must operate according to clearly defined constraints. Incremental testing therefore allows failures in generation, verification, and rewriting to be distinguished.

Then, the research contribution itself may require refinement after pilot evaluation. For example, the definition of learner-relative cognitive mismatch may need adjustment if initial testing shows that the verifier is overly strict or too permissive. An iterative development cycle supports evidence-driven refinement without changing the overall research objective.

#### Development cycle

A repeated development cycle will be used throughout implementation:

**Design → Implement → Test → Evaluate → Refine → Integrate**

#### Role of Agile principles

The project does not require a strict implementation of a full Scrum framework. Instead, Agile principles will be adopted through short development iterations, incremental delivery of functional modules, continuous testing, and adaptation based on experimental evidence and supervisor/team feedback.

The selected development approach is therefore:

**Iterative prototyping supported by Agile development principles, with modular development and continuous experimental validation.**

### 3.7 Technology Selection and Justification

The technologies used in Component 4 were selected by comparing candidate options against the requirements of the verification task rather than by adopting defaults. This section states the alternatives considered, the basis for selection, and the risks accepted in each case.

#### 3.7.1 Explanation Generator

Three options were considered for the generation stage: a proprietary hosted model accessed by API (GPT-4o, Claude), an open-weight instruction-tuned model deployed locally (Llama- or Mistral-family), and a smaller fine-tuned model trained on educational explanation data.

GPT-4o was selected for the primary experiments. Three factors determined the choice. First, the research contribution is the verification layer, not the generator; a strong, widely reported generator makes the baseline harder to beat and therefore makes any measured improvement more credible than one obtained against a deliberately weak model. Second, the same model is used in both conditions, so generator capability is held constant and cannot explain the observed difference. Third, the wider project already uses GPT-4o for concept and relation extraction in Component 1, so a single API dependency serves the whole middleware and reduces cost and integration effort.

The principal risk accepted is non-determinism and version drift: hosted models are updated without notice, which threatens reproducibility. This is mitigated by pinning the model version string where the API permits it, fixing generation parameters across conditions, recording the model identifier with every generated sample, and retaining all generated explanations as stored artifacts rather than regenerating them for later analysis.

A fine-tuned educational model was rejected because no suitably sized annotated corpus of DSA explanations exists and constructing one would displace the research contribution. An open-weight model is retained as the secondary generalization condition described in Section 3.3, which tests whether the verification mechanism remains effective when the generator changes.

#### 3.7.2 Factual Verification Mechanism

Four mechanisms for claim-level factual verification were evaluated against the literature reviewed in Section 1.2.3.

| Mechanism | Basis | Strength | Limitation for this task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuned NLI encoder (e.g. DeBERTa-v3-MNLI) | Entailment classification between premise and hypothesis | Deterministic, inexpensive, independently evaluable, no external API dependency | Requires graph evidence to be verbalized into premise text; performance is granularity-sensitive [11] |
| LLM-as-judge | Prompt a generative model to assess support | Handles varied phrasing; no verbalization step | The generator would partly judge itself; non-deterministic; costly at claim volume |
| Self-consistency sampling | Agreement across stochastic samples [10] | Requires no external knowledge source | Measures model confidence, not truth; cannot use the knowledge graph, which is the available authority here |
| Deterministic graph matching | Direct Cypher queries against typed edges | Exact, fully traceable, zero inference cost | Only handles claims that map cleanly to a represented relation |

*Table 7 - Comparison of Candidate Factual-Verification Mechanisms*

A hybrid of deterministic graph matching and NLI-based entailment was selected. Claims that map directly onto a represented relation (for example, that one concept is a prerequisite of another) are resolved by query, which is exact and produces a traceable evidence reference for the faithfulness report. Claims expressed in free-form explanatory language, where no single edge corresponds to the assertion, are routed to the NLI verifier with the relevant verbalized subgraph as premise.

This division follows directly from the literature. SummaC demonstrates that entailment accuracy over long inputs depends strongly on the granularity at which premise and hypothesis are compared [11], which supports pairing individual atomic claims with a narrow retrieved subgraph rather than an entire explanation with an entire graph. QAFactEval further shows that entailment-based and question-answering-based signals are complementary rather than redundant [16], indicating that no single verification mechanism should be assumed sufficient. Self-consistency was rejected as a primary mechanism because the research explicitly requires an external authority: as argued in Section 1.2.3, a model's agreement with itself cannot establish agreement with the verified educational knowledge base.

The residual risk is that a claim is genuinely supported but the retrieved subgraph is insufficient, producing a false unsupported label. This is why verifier precision and recall are measured against manual annotation (Section 3.5.1) rather than assumed.

#### 3.7.3 Claim Decomposition

Sentence-level splitting and atomic-fact decomposition were compared. Atomic decomposition, following the FActScore approach [9], was selected. A single explanatory sentence in a DSA context frequently carries several independent assertions. A definitional claim, a complexity claim and a prerequisite claim can occupy one sentence, so sentence-level verification would assign one label to all three, concealing a violation inside otherwise correct content. Since the entire research premise is that a factually correct claim can coexist with a cognitively inappropriate one in the same passage, verification granularity finer than the sentence is a requirement rather than a refinement.

The cost accepted is additional LLM calls for decomposition, and the possibility that decomposition itself introduces error. Decomposition quality is therefore treated as a distinct development stage subject to pilot testing (Section 3.6) rather than assumed correct.

#### 3.7.4 Bloom-Level Classification

Two approaches were considered for estimating the cognitive demand of generated content: prompting a general-purpose LLM, and a fine-tuned encoder classifier.

The evidence favours the fine-tuned approach. Almatrafi and Johri compared several GPT-4 prompting strategies against a fine-tuned BERT classifier for Bloom categorization of learning outcomes and found that the fine-tuned classifier outperformed the strongest prompt-based configuration [13]. Their finding has a direct design consequence for this research: the cognitive verifier will not determine appropriateness by asking a generative model whether its own explanation is simple enough. A verification mechanism whose judgements are produced by the same class of model that produced the content offers no independent check.

The selected approach uses Bloom-level properties represented explicitly in the knowledge graph as the primary source of cognitive demand, supplemented where necessary by a separately evaluated classifier rather than by generative self-assessment. Because the study by Almatrafi and Johri also shows that automated Bloom classification is imperfect even at its best, classification reliability is measured against manual annotation and reported alongside verifier performance rather than treated as ground truth.

**Implemented estimator and its measured reliability.** The prototype uses an inspectable verb-cue estimator, applied to learner tasks (questions and instructions) so that ordinary statements are not read as demanding tasks. It is scored against a labelled item set rather than assumed correct; on the current single-annotator items it reaches 100% on the in-vocabulary test split but only 16.7% on indirectly phrased items, which shows the limit of any verb-cue matcher and motivates the classifier above.

#### 3.7.5 Knowledge Store

Neo4j with Cypher was compared against an RDF triple store with SPARQL and against an in-memory graph library such as NetworkX.

Neo4j was selected, principally because it is already the storage layer for the shared pedagogy-typed graph produced by Component 1, and Component 4's requirement is to consume that graph rather than to construct a second representation. Beyond this constraint, the labelled-property-graph model is well matched to the data: pedagogical edges in this schema carry properties such as prerequisite strength and cognitive load, which a property graph represents natively but which RDF represents only through reification. Cypher's variable-length path expressions also express prerequisite-chain traversal concisely. This is the operation underlying unmastered-prerequisite detection. An in-memory library was rejected because the graph is shared concurrently across four components and must persist between sessions.

#### 3.7.6 Integration Layer

REST with JSON was selected over gRPC and direct database access. The components are developed independently and in different runtime environments, and the middleware is intended for third-party integration by LMS and MOOC platforms, for which REST and JSON impose the lowest adoption barrier. gRPC offers better performance but adds schema-compilation coupling between components developed on separate timelines. Direct database access was rejected because it would couple Component 4 to Component 1's internal schema rather than to a stable published interface.

#### 3.7.7 Summary of Selections

| Function | Selected | Principal alternatives rejected | Basis for selection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explanation generation | GPT-4o (API) | Open-weight local model; fine-tuned educational model | Strong baseline; held constant across conditions; shared project dependency |
| Factual verification | Hybrid Cypher matching + NLI entailment | LLM-as-judge; self-consistency | External authority required; granularity control [11]; traceable evidence |
| Claim decomposition | Atomic decomposition | Sentence-level splitting | Multiple assertions per sentence conceal violations [9] |
| Bloom classification | Graph-represented Bloom properties + evaluated classifier | Generative self-assessment | Fine-tuned classifiers outperform prompting [13]; independence from generator |
| Knowledge store | Neo4j / Cypher | RDF/SPARQL; NetworkX | Shared C1 dependency; native edge properties; path traversal |
| Integration | REST / JSON | gRPC; direct DB access | Third-party adoption; loose coupling |

## 4. HIGH-LEVEL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

### 4.1 Overview of the Proposed Solution

The proposed system is a knowledge-graph-grounded middleware framework for verified personalized learning-path generation and explanation. The complete solution consists of four research components connected through a shared pedagogy-typed knowledge graph and common integration interfaces.

The overall architecture follows the sequence:

**Component 1 → Component 2 → Component 3 → Component 4**

while the knowledge graph produced by Component 1 is also directly consumed by the downstream components. The purpose of this design is to ensure that learner modeling, learning-path generation, and explanation generation operate over a consistent pedagogical representation rather than functioning as isolated modules. The shared knowledge graph functions as the common symbolic substrate of the complete middleware.

At a high level:

- Component 1 constructs and stores the verified pedagogy-typed DSA knowledge graph.
- Component 2 uses the graph together with learner interaction information to estimate concept-level mastery and uncertainty.
- Component 3 uses the graph and learner state to generate and verify a personalized learning path.
- Component 4 generates an explanation for the verified path and validates that explanation for factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness.

The final system therefore produces both a verified personalized learning path and a verified personalized explanation rather than relying directly on unconstrained LLM generation.

### 4.2 Overall Component Integration

The four research components form a sequential but interconnected pipeline.

**Component 1 - Pedagogy-Typed Knowledge Graph.** Component 1 provides the shared structural knowledge representation used throughout the system. The graph contains DSA concepts together with pedagogically meaningful information including prerequisite relationships, Bloom-related information, cognitive-load information, and misconception-related relationships. The verified graph is stored in Neo4j and exposed to the downstream components. For Component 4, the important dependency is that C1 provides the knowledge and pedagogical evidence against which generated explanation claims can be checked.

**Component 2 - Learner-State Modeling.** Component 2 produces a learner-state representation over the concept graph. The output consists primarily of concept-level mastery together with uncertainty estimates. Its output is consumed by the path planner and also passed to Component 4 for learner-relative cognitive verification. Component 4 does not calculate mastery itself. It uses the learner-state information supplied by C2 to determine which concepts the learner has sufficiently mastered and to support the derivation of the learner's current cognitive constraint.

**Component 3 - Verified Learning-Path Generation.** Component 3 generates the personalized sequence of DSA concepts that the learner should follow toward a target learning goal, using an LLM proposer constrained by a symbolic critic, and verifies that sequence before release. Its output is the verified learning path consumed by Component 4. Component 4 does not change the ordering of concepts or create additional learning paths. Its responsibility begins after the path has been verified.

**Component 4 - Bloom-Level-Aware Faithfulness Verification.** Component 4 is the individual research focus of this proposal. It receives:

- C1: pedagogy-typed knowledge graph and Bloom-related information
- C2: learner mastery state
- C3: verified learning path.

It then performs the following processing stages:

**Input context → LLM explanation generation → Atomic-claim extraction → Factual verification → Cognitive verification → Violation detection → Targeted rewriting → Final verified explanation**

The final outputs are:

- A verified personalized explanation
- Inline knowledge-graph citations or grounding references; and
- A faithfulness report describing detected and corrected violations.

Component 4 is structured as a linear pipeline of seven stages operating between the upstream component outputs and the learner-facing response. The stages are separable modules, consistent with the modularity requirement NFR10, so that each can be developed, tested, and replaced independently.

**Stage 1: Context assembly** - The component receives the verified learning path from Component 3, the learner state from Component 2, and a query interface to the pedagogy-typed knowledge graph from Component 1. For the current path step, the relevant concept subgraph is retrieved, comprising the target concept, its prerequisite chain, associated Bloom-level properties, and any recorded misconception relations. The learner's Bloom ceiling is derived at this stage from the mastery estimates of the concepts in that subgraph, and the set of sufficiently mastered concepts is established.

**Stage 2: Explanation generation** - The assembled context is passed to GPT-4o, which produces a draft explanation of the current path step. The draft is not delivered to the learner; it is the input to verification. The same generation process, with the same context, constitutes the experimental baseline when verification is bypassed.

**Stage 3: Atomic-claim decomposition** - The draft is decomposed into atomic claims, each carrying a single verifiable assertion, together with a reference to its position in the source text so that any correction can later be applied in place rather than by regenerating the passage.

**Stage 4: Factual verification** - Each claim is matched to the relevant graph evidence and classified as supported, contradicted, or unsupported, using the hybrid mechanism described in Section 3.7.2. Supported claims retain a reference to the graph evidence that supported them, which subsequently populates the inline knowledge-graph citations in the final output.

**Stage 5: Cognitive verification** - Claims surviving factual verification are evaluated against the learner constraint established in Stage 1. A claim is a cognitive violation if it requires a cognitive operation above the learner's Bloom ceiling, or if it depends on prerequisite concepts the learner has not sufficiently mastered. The current learning target is exempt from the second condition, since it is by definition new to the learner. Factual verification is necessary but not sufficient: a claim must clear both stages before it is treated as faithful.

**Stage 6: Violation typing and targeted rewriting** - Detected violations are recorded as typed records distinguishing factual violations from cognitive violations, with cognitive violations further separated into Bloom-level mismatch and unmastered-prerequisite dependence. Only the affected claims are rewritten or grounded; valid content is preserved unchanged. Rewritten claims re-enter Stages 4 and 5, and may pass through a bounded number of correction iterations before being accepted or escalated.

**Stage 7: Output assembly** - The verified explanation is reassembled from validated and corrected claims, annotated with inline knowledge-graph citations and per-claim confidence tiers, and returned together with a faithfulness report recording every detected violation, its type, and its correction status.

The architecture makes the research contribution structurally visible. Stages 4 and 5 are separate by design rather than merged into a single quality assessment, which is what permits the ablation study in Section 3.5.3 to isolate the contribution of cognitive verification from that of factual verification.

### 4.4 Planned Integration Method

Integration among the four research components will use the project's shared REST API and JSON-based interfaces. The middleware packaging, REST API, JSON schemas, and sample integration client are shared engineering deliverables of the research team rather than the research contribution of any individual member.

For Component 4, the planned integration flow is:

1. Retrieve the required knowledge-graph context from C1/Neo4j.
2. Receive the learner-state representation from C2.
3. Receive the verified learning path from C3.
4. Pass the combined structured context to the C4 explanation-generation and verification pipeline.
5. Return the verified explanation and associated faithfulness metadata through the middleware API.

The JSON interfaces should include sufficient identifiers to associate concepts appearing in the learning path and explanation with their corresponding knowledge-graph nodes. The final schema will be agreed during system integration and is therefore not finalized at proposal stage.

### 5.2 Functional Requirements

Functional requirements specify the operations that Component 4 must perform to fulfil its research objective.

**FR1: Receive Upstream Context** — The system shall receive and process the verified learning path, learner mastery state, and relevant pedagogy-typed knowledge-graph information provided by Components 1–3.

**FR2: Generate Personalized Explanation** — The system shall generate a natural-language explanation for the verified DSA learning path using GPT-4o and the available learner context. The explanation should primarily explain the selected learning path and provide sufficient concept-level explanation to make the recommendation understandable.

**FR3: Decompose the Explanation into Atomic Claims** — The system shall decompose the generated explanation into atomic claims so that individual factual and pedagogical statements can be independently verified.

**FR4: Retrieve Relevant Knowledge-Graph Evidence** — For each factual claim, the system shall retrieve the relevant concepts and relationships from the pedagogy-typed knowledge graph required to assess that claim.

**FR5: Verify Factual Entailment** — The system shall verify generated factual claims against relevant knowledge-graph evidence using the planned hybrid NLI and rule-based verification mechanism. Claims shall be identifiable as, at minimum:

- Supported/entailed
- Contradicted or
- Unsupported.

**FR6: Verify Cognitive Appropriateness** — The system shall evaluate whether a generated claim is cognitively appropriate for the current learner using:

- Bloom-related information
- Learner concept mastery
- Prerequisite relationships and
- The current target/path context.

A factually valid claim may therefore still be classified as a cognitive violation.

**FR7: Identify Unmastered-Prerequisite Violations** — The system shall identify explanatory content that relies on prerequisite concepts that the learner has not sufficiently mastered. The current learning target itself shall not automatically be treated as a violation merely because it is new to the learner.

**FR8: Classify Faithfulness Violations** — The system shall distinguish between at least:

- Factual faithfulness violations, and
- Cognitive-appropriateness violations.

Where appropriate, cognitive violations may be further categorized according to Bloom-level mismatch or dependence on unmastered prerequisite concepts.

**FR9: Perform Targeted Rewriting** — When a violation is detected, the system shall rewrite or appropriately ground the affected content rather than unnecessarily regenerating the entire explanation. This requirement supports both efficiency and preservation of already valid content.

**FR10: Re-verify Corrected Content** — Rewritten content shall pass through the relevant verification stages again before being accepted as part of the final explanation.

**FR11: Produce Verified Explanation** — The system shall return a final verified personalized explanation after factual and cognitive constraints have been satisfied according to the implemented verification rules.

**FR12: Provide Knowledge-Graph Grounding References** — The system shall associate relevant explanation content with the knowledge-graph evidence used during verification, enabling inline KG citations or equivalent grounding references.

**FR13: Assign Verification Confidence Tiers** — The system shall assign a verification-confidence tier to each evaluated claim according to the strength of its factual evidence and the certainty of its cognitive-appropriateness decision. Three tiers are proposed:

- **High:** The claim is directly supported by an explicit knowledge-graph relation or property, and the relevant learner-state evidence is clearly within the defined acceptance constraints.
- **Medium:** The claim is supported through inference over multiple graph relations or the cognitive decision is valid but lies close to a defined mastery or classification threshold.
- **Low / Inconclusive:** Available evidence is insufficient to accept the claim confidently. A Low-confidence claim shall not be included in the final verified explanation without further processing. It must be rewritten and re-verified, escalated for review, or omitted from the final response.

The confidence tier therefore represents the strength of verification evidence rather than permission to deliver unverified content. All claims included in the final verified explanation must satisfy the implemented factual and cognitive verification requirements.

**FR14: Generate a Faithfulness Report** — The system shall generate a structured faithfulness report indicating detected factual and cognitive violations and their correction status. The report shall include the confidence tier assigned to each claim.

**FR15: Maintain the Demonstrated Bloom Level** — The system shall keep, for each learner and concept, the Bloom level the learner has demonstrated and the history of check-question results, and shall update it after each answered check question. A failed task above the demonstrated level shall not lower it.

**FR16: Derive and Verify the Target Level** — The system shall derive a target Bloom level for the next explanation (the level above the demonstrated level when the concept's prerequisites are mastered, otherwise the demonstrated level), shall treat content above it as a Bloom mismatch, and shall treat an explanation with no check question at the target level as a stagnation violation that is repaired and reported.

**FR17: Provide Contingent Scaffolding and Check Questions** — The system shall end each explanation with a check question at the target level and shall reduce supporting detail as the learner passes consecutive check questions and restore it after a failure.

### 5.4 User Requirements and Component Design

The identified requirements directly influence the Component 4 architecture. Learner usability motivates personalized explanation generation and the planned user evaluation. Reliability requirements motivate the separation of generation from verification instead of depending entirely on the LLM's initial output. The requirement for factual trustworthiness leads to knowledge-graph-based entailment checking, while the requirement for learner suitability motivates Bloom- and mastery-aware cognitive verification.

Efficiency considerations motivate atomic targeted correction, where only content responsible for a detected violation is rewritten. This avoids unnecessary full-response regeneration and supports the modular architecture suggested during stakeholder and panel feedback. Scalability considerations similarly favour separating explanation generation, factual verification, cognitive verification, and rewriting into independently maintainable modules.

Privacy requirements arise because Component 4 consumes learner-state information from Component 2. Learner assessment and interaction information may contain sensitive data and must be protected during storage and processing. Consequently, the C4 implementation will operate on the minimum learner-state information required for personalization and verification and will avoid transmitting unnecessary personal identifiers to external LLM services.

### 5.5 Limitations of the Requirements-Elicitation Process

The requirements identified at proposal stage are preliminary. Peer discussions were informal and involved a small convenience group of approximately 5–10 undergraduate students rather than a structured requirements survey. Consequently, these discussions are used only as contextual evidence of current AI-tool usage and are not treated as statistically representative evidence of the wider student population. The requirements are therefore primarily derived from stakeholder/expert feedback, the approved system architecture, the research objectives, and findings from the literature review. Further usability evidence will be collected during the planned Component 4 learner evaluation.

## 6. COMMERCIALIZATION PLAN

### 6.1 Target Market and Customer Persona

The proposed solution is positioned as a middleware service for AI-supported personalized learning, rather than as a complete replacement for an existing Learning Management System. Its primary commercial customers would therefore be organizations that already provide or manage digital learning environments and require more trustworthy personalized-learning capabilities.

The principal target markets are:

- Universities and higher-education institutions
- LMS and MOOC providers
- EdTech organizations
- Corporate learning and development platforms.

The proposed solution does not address the digital-learning market as a whole. It targets the narrower segment of platforms that already deliver digital courses, have adopted or are adopting AI-assisted tutoring, and require verifiable control over the factual and pedagogical correctness of what that tutoring produces. Within this segment, the relevant purchasing unit is the platform or institution rather than the individual learner, since integration decisions are made at product or curriculum level.

Evidence that this segment is commercially active is already visible. Coursera reported in 2024 that its Coach assistant had supported more than one million learners, and that its use was associated with a 9.5% higher first-attempt quiz pass rate and 11.6% more lessons completed per hour [5]. Khan Academy has similarly deployed AI tutoring across its platform at scale [3]. These deployments demonstrate both institutional willingness to adopt AI tutoring and, through their own published cautions regarding model error [4], an unmet need for the verification capability the proposed middleware provides.

The primary end users are learners, while the direct purchasing or integration decisions are more likely to be made by institutional customers such as LMS administrators, EdTech product teams, course-platform operators, and educational technology managers.

For the research prototype, the educational domain is limited to Data Structures and Algorithms, with undergraduate university students as the principal learner group. However, the wider middleware architecture is intended to be reusable across other educational domains if an appropriate pedagogy-typed knowledge graph is available. The final outcome is therefore reusable middleware that can be integrated with LMS, MOOC, or corporate training platforms.

#### Proposed Customer Persona

A representative institutional customer can be characterized as:

*An LMS or EdTech product team responsible for delivering digital courses to a large learner population and seeking to add personalized AI tutoring while maintaining greater control over prerequisite correctness, factual grounding, and learner-appropriate explanations.*

The customer would value an integration that can operate through APIs without requiring the institution to independently develop a knowledge graph, learner model, learning-path verifier, and explanation-faithfulness layer.

### 6.2 Value Proposition

The principal value proposition of the complete research framework is:

*To enable learning platforms to provide personalized, prerequisite-aware and explainable learning support while reducing factual and pedagogical errors associated with unconstrained LLM generation.*

For Component 4 specifically, the value proposition is narrower:

*To provide learner-facing AI explanations that are checked not only for factual support but also for cognitive suitability to the individual learner before delivery.*

This differentiates the proposed component from ordinary LLM-generated explanations. Rather than assuming that personalization in the prompt guarantees an appropriate response, Component 4 performs explicit post-generation verification using the project's structured educational knowledge and learner-state information.

The customer value therefore includes:

- Greater trustworthiness of AI-generated explanations
- Transparent identification of factual and cognitive violations
- Personalized explanatory support based on learner mastery
- Reusable API-based integration with existing learning platforms
- Reduced need for institutions to develop the complete verification pipeline internally.

The use of a single pedagogy-typed knowledge graph across learner modeling, path generation, and explanation verification is one of the main architectural differentiators of the complete system.

### 6.3 Revenue Model

Three commercialization approaches are proposed.

**SaaS Licensing** — Institutions could subscribe to the middleware through a recurring Software-as-a-Service licensing model. Different tiers could be defined according to factors such as learner volume, API usage, supported courses, or required deployment capacity.

**Usage-Based Pricing** — A usage-based model could charge customers according to:

- Active learners
- Number of generated and verified explanations
- API requests or
- Monthly processing volume.

This model may be particularly suitable for smaller institutions or course providers whose usage varies over time.

**Integration and Customization Services** — Additional revenue could be generated by providing:

- LMS integration support
- Domain-specific knowledge-graph onboarding
- API customization
- Deployment support
- Institutional configuration and technical consulting.

This approach would be particularly relevant when organizations require the middleware to operate with existing internal learning systems or domain-specific educational content.

A hybrid SaaS + usage-based model is likely the most appropriate long-term direction, while integration consulting could operate as an additional service.

- **Enterprise/custom tier:** negotiated pricing for private deployment, large learner populations, additional domains, or custom integration.

The final pricing model would need to account for API inference costs, cloud infrastructure, support costs, desired margin, and competitor pricing before commercialization.

### 6.6 Competitive Advantage

As established in the literature review, existing commercial platforms already provide AI-assisted tutoring, learner personalization, and content grounding. None of these capabilities alone constitutes a competitive advantage. The differentiation of the proposed solution lies instead in the integration and transparency of its verification layer.

The key differentiating characteristics are:

1. **Shared pedagogical knowledge substrate:** learner modeling, path generation, and explanation verification operate against a common pedagogy-typed knowledge graph.
2. **Dual explanation verification:** explanations are evaluated for both factual entailment and learner-relative cognitive appropriateness.
3. **Explicit cognitive-faithfulness violations:** a statement can be treated as problematic even when factually correct if it exceeds the learner's applicable cognitive constraint.
4. **Typed violation reporting:** factual and cognitive failures can be reported separately rather than reduced to a single opaque quality score.
5. **Targeted correction:** violating content can be selectively rewritten instead of regenerating an entire otherwise valid explanation.
6. **API-oriented middleware design:** the solution is intended to integrate with existing educational platforms instead of requiring institutions to replace their current LMS.

### 6.7 Intellectual Property Considerations

The proposed research produces four categories of intellectual property.

**Software implementation.** The reference implementation of the middleware, including the Component 4 verification pipeline, is intended for release under a permissive open-source licence such as the MIT Licence or Apache Licence 2.0. Permissive licensing supports academic reproducibility and allows commercial integrators to adopt the middleware without the obligations imposed by copyleft licences, which is consistent with the intended API-based integration model. Apache 2.0 is the preferred option because it includes an explicit patent grant.

**Architectural design.** The neuro-symbolic verification architecture, specifically the combination of claim-level factual entailment against a pedagogy-typed graph with learner-relative cognitive verification and typed violation reporting, may constitute protectable subject matter. Whether it satisfies the applicable patentability requirements will be assessed with SLIIT's research and innovation office before any public disclosure that could affect novelty. No patent claim is asserted at proposal stage.

**Research datasets and evaluation assets.** The hand-annotated gold corpus, the Component 4 evaluation corpus, the claim-level ground-truth annotations, and the user-study instruments are produced by the research team using institutional resources. Ownership of these assets will rest with the research team and SLIIT under the applicable institutional research policy. Release for academic reuse will be considered after the dissertation is examined.

**Interface specifications.** The inter-component JSON schemas and the REST API specification are shared engineering deliverables of the project. These will be documented and registered as project outputs under the SLIIT Centre of Excellence for AI research cluster.

**Third-party dependencies.** The implementation depends on external services and libraries with their own licence terms, including the OpenAI API, Neo4j, and Python-based machine-learning libraries. The licence conditions of each dependency will be reviewed before any commercial deployment, since the terms applicable to research use may differ from those applicable to a commercial service.

### 7.2 Justification of Each Expense

**Closed-weight LLM API credits (Item 1).** The proposer in Component 3 and the explanation generator in Component 4 call a closed-weight LLM. During experiments, each path or explanation is generated many times across conditions (proposer-only vs. proposer-plus-critic, multiple target concepts, repeated runs for reliability), so a credit budget is needed. Cost is controllable by limiting experiment scale and caching results.

**GPU / cloud compute (Item 2).** Component 2's GNN, the open-weight LLM, and Component 3's offline-RL policy require GPU time to train and run. Renting cloud GPU hours is far cheaper than purchasing hardware for a one-year project and avoids maintenance cost.

**Cloud hosting (Item 3).** The middleware is delivered as a REST API, which must be hosted so the four components can be integration-tested end-to-end. A modest cloud instance is sufficient for prototype-scale testing.

**Storage (Item 4).** Storage needs are small: per-learner mastery vectors are only a few kilobytes each, and the knowledge graph for one domain is lightweight. The cost mainly covers datasets and logs.

**Gold-corpus annotation (Item 5).** Component 1's evaluation depends on a hand annotated gold corpus, with inter-annotator agreement reported. This is real human labor and is the main non-compute cost; it may involve compensating a second annotator.

**Software licenses (Item 6).** The project uses open-source libraries (Python, PyTorch/PyG, etc.) wherever possible, so licensing cost is minimal; a small allowance covers any paid utility.

**Human effort (Item 7).** The primary development resource is the four students' time. As is standard for undergraduate research, this is recorded as an in-kind contribution rather than a cash cost, but it is the largest real investment in the project.

**Contingency (Item 8).** A contingency of roughly 10% is included to absorb additional experiment runs, API overruns, or unforeseen expenses, standard practice for a research budget.
