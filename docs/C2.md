## ABSTRACT

A key limitation of current online learning platforms is their inability to combine dependable pedagogical structure with individualized learning. Traditional rule-based approaches ensure prerequisite compliance but follow relatively rigid pathways, whereas LLM-driven approaches provide dynamic personalization without sufficient mechanisms for validating their generated content and learning sequences. My individual contribution (Component 2) targets a narrower but consequential problem inside knowledge tracing itself. Existing graph-based knowledge-tracing models such as GKT and GIKT output a single mastery score per concept, optimized only for ranking accuracy (AUC), a mastery estimate built on twenty observed answers and one built on two both surface as the same number, even though a downstream planner should trust them very differently. This is my identified research gap, no existing knowledge-tracing model tells a consuming system how much to trust a given mastery estimate.

I propose closing this gap by extending a graph neural network (GNN) student model with Monte Carlo Dropout, so each concept receives both a mastery score and a calibrated uncertainty score from repeated stochastic forward passes, with cold-start learners handled by propagating priors along the prerequisite graph built by Component 1. The research follows a quantitative, experimental methodology: the model is trained and evaluated on the public ASSISTments benchmark, benchmarked against GKT and GIKT baselines, and assessed using AUC alongside Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score, with a dedicated ablation isolating the benefit of the cold-start priors. The GNN architecture and the Monte Carlo Dropout sampling parameter are treated as design choices to be justified empirically rather than assumed. Component 2 sits between the knowledge graph and the path planner in the wider system: its calibrated uncertainty output is the signal the planner uses to decide whether to trust a mastery estimate or insert a verification step before advancing the learner, making trustworthy learner modelling a precondition for safe personalization.

**Keywords:** knowledge tracing, graph neural networks, Monte Carlo Dropout, uncertainty calibration, cold-start learner modelling

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Background and Context

Every online learning platform available today is forced into a tradeoff that, as far as I have found in the literature, nobody has actually resolved. Rule based and ontology-based adaptive systems enforce prerequisites correctly and are explainable, but they give every student essentially the same path regardless of what that specific student actually knows. LLM-based tutors go the other way, they personalize fluently and can hold a natural conversation, but they can hallucinate facts, skip prerequisites a student has not actually mastered, and give no way for anyone to verify the path or explanation before it reaches the learner [9], [10].

This affects two groups directly. Learners - particularly those outside well resourced institutions who rely on free or low cost platforms rather than a human tutor are the ones who absorb the cost of an unverified system, either being pushed into material they are not ready for or wasting time re-covering material they already know. Instructors and institutions are the second group. They lack tooling to check whether an AI-generated path or explanation is pedagogically sound before it is shown to a student, so they either trust the system blindly or fall back to manually re-sequencing content themselves.

The scale of this is not small. The global e-learning market is projected to reach roughly USD 400 billion by 2026, and a large share of that growth is specifically in adaptive and AI-personalised platforms, which means whatever trust problem exists in today's systems will only be deployed to more learners, not fewer, unless it is addressed directly.

Existing solutions sit at one of the two extremes described above, and neither is sufficient on its own. My own component does not attempt to solve the whole problem. It targets one specific piece of it. Knowledge tracing models such as GKT and GIKT, which estimate what a student knows using a graph structure, still collapse that estimate into a single score with no signal for how much that score should be trusted [1], [2]. My proposed contribution is to extend this kind of model so that it reports a calibrated confidence alongside every mastery estimate, which is one of the four pieces alongside knowledge-graph construction, path planning, and explanation generation, that together form the GenAir system my group is building.

This research aligns with UN Sustainable Development Goal 4 (Quality Education), specifically Target 4.4 on increasing the number of people with relevant skills, by aiming to make personalised, prerequisite-correct learning guidance verifiable and scalable rather than dependent on either a human instructor's time or an unverified AI system. Within SLIIT, the project falls under the Centre of Excellence for AI (CoEAI) research cluster, in the EdTech / e-learning industry vertical.

### 1.2 Literature Review

This review focuses on two bodies of work that Component 2 sits directly between graph-based and sequence-based knowledge-tracing models that estimate learner mastery, and uncertainty quantification techniques from the broader deep learning literature that I am adapting into that setting.

#### 1.2.1 Graph Based Knowledge Tracing - GKT and GIKT

GKT models each concept as a node in a graph and uses a graph neural network to update related concepts whenever a student answers a question, rather than treating each concept as independent [1]. It was evaluated on knowledge-tracing benchmarks and showed that incorporating concept relationships improves next item prediction accuracy (AUC) over non graph baselines. GIKT extends this by also linking questions to concepts directly and adding a recap mechanism that re-attends to related past interactions before predicting [2]. Both report strong AUC figures, and both are the closest architectural relatives to what I am proposing, since I also build a GNN over a concept graph. Their shared limitation, and the one this component targets directly, is that both output a single point estimate mastery score with no accompanying measure of how confident that estimate is a limitation the original papers do not discuss, since they optimise for AUC alone.

#### 1.2.2 Sequence Based Knowledge Tracing - DKT

Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) uses a recurrent neural network over a student's interaction sequence, without any explicit graph structure [3]. It is included as the lineage GKT and GIKT build on, and as the sequence-modelling half of my baseline comparison. Its limitation is the one GKT and GIKT already identified, no prerequisite structure so I treat it as background rather than a primary baseline.

#### 1.2.3 Uncertainty Quantification: Monte Carlo Dropout and Alternatives

Gal and Ghahramani show that keeping dropout active at inference and sampling multiple stochastic forward passes approximates Bayesian posterior uncertainty, without the cost of a full Bayesian neural network [4] the mechanism I propose attaching to the GNN. I checked this against its main alternative, deep ensembles, which train several independently-initialized models and treat their disagreement as the uncertainty signal [5], ensembles can give slightly better calibration, but at a training cost that multiplies with ensemble size disproportionate for a single component. On the number of passes, the literature does not converge on one value: published work uses anywhere from 10 to over 100 depending on task and latency tolerance, so 30 is a starting point I will justify with my own sensitivity analysis rather than treat as settled.

#### 1.2.4 Calibration as a Distinct Evaluation Practice

Guo et al. show that modern neural networks are frequently overconfident, and that accuracy and calibration are genuinely different properties requiring separate measurement, via tools such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE) [6]. This is the direct source of my evaluation approach: none of the knowledge-tracing papers above report a calibration metric at all, which is itself evidence for the gap I am addressing.

#### 1.2.5 Existing Products and Systems

Commercially deployed adaptive tutors, such as Khanmigo, personalize pacing and explanations using an LLM, and are used by a large number of learners already. However, as closed commercial systems they do not publish a mastery-estimation methodology, a calibration figure, or a prerequisite-violation rate, so their internal approach cannot be verified or compared against academically. This is consistent with what my team's shared literature review found across the wider GenAir project: personalization quality is visible to users, but the trustworthiness of the model behind it is not which is exactly the property my component is designed to expose and measure.

### 1.3 Research Gap

Graph-based knowledge tracing models (GKT, GIKT) demonstrate that relational structure improves mastery prediction, and separately, the uncertainty quantification literature (Monte Carlo Dropout, calibration metrics) demonstrates how to attach and evaluate a trustworthy confidence signal to a neural network's output, but no published knowledge tracing model combines the two. The specific gap this component investigates is, existing knowledge tracing models report what a learner is predicted to know, but never how much that prediction should be trusted, which leaves any downstream system that consumes the score with no way to distinguish a well-supported estimate from a shaky one.

This gap matters beyond the two papers it is drawn from. Across the wider knowledge tracing literature, mastery estimation and confidence estimation have developed as two separate research threads that have not yet been combined for this task: graph based models (GKT, GIKT) optimize purely for ranking accuracy and were never evaluated for calibration, sequence models (DKT) share the same blind spot, and generic uncertainty quantification techniques (Monte Carlo Dropout, deep ensembles) have been validated on image classification and general regression tasks, not on a graph-structured, pedagogically typed knowledge tracing setting where the consumer of the uncertainty is a downstream planner rather than a human analyst. The table below summarizes, for each relevant line of prior work, precisely why it does not close this gap on its own, and which part of my proposed design is intended to close it.

| Existing Approach | Why It Fails for Calibrated Knowledge Tracing | Gap Addressed by This Project |
|---|---|---|
| GKT / GIKT (graph-based KT) [1], [2] | Model concept relationships via a GNN, but output a single point estimate mastery score with no confidence signal. | Adds a calibrated uncertainty score alongside mastery, from the same GNN. |
| DKT (sequence-based KT) [3] | No graph structure and no calibration establishes the sequence modelling baseline this project extends beyond. | Retained only as a secondary reference point, not a target architecture. |

| Cold-start handling in recommender systems (background) | Content based cold-start techniques exist for recommenders, but do not transfer directly to a typed, pedagogical prerequisite graph. | Cold-start priors are derived specifically from Component 1's typed prerequisite edges, and isolated via a dedicated ablation (Table 1). |

## 2. OBJECTIVE

### 2.1. Main Objective

To design and evaluate a graph neural network based learner model that produces calibrated mastery and uncertainty estimates for every concept a learner has interacted with, and to demonstrate a measurable improvement in calibration quality (Expected Calibration Error) over an uncalibrated knowledge tracing baseline on the ASSISTments dataset.

### 2.2. Specific Objectives

- Ingest raw learner interaction data from the ASSISTments benchmark and engineer per concept features (rolling accuracy, attempt count, recency weighted correctness) suitable as GNN node input.
- Design and train a graph neural network over the concept graph supplied by Component 1, using message passing so that a concept's predicted mastery is informed by its prerequisite neighbors.
- Implement Monte Carlo Dropout at inference time to produce a calibrated uncertainty estimate alongside every mastery score and empirically justify the number of stochastic passes through a dedicated sensitivity study rather than adopting a default value.
- Implement a cold-start mechanism that propagates mastery priors along prerequisite edges for learners with little or no interaction history.
- Evaluate the model against GKT and GIKT baselines using AUC for predictive accuracy and ECE / Brier score for calibration quality, including an ablation isolating the contribution of the cold-start priors.
- Investigate alternative graph representation architectures beyond a standard GNN (e.g. attention-based and relation typed message passing) to determine whether a more expressive graph representation improves calibration or efficiency, since this is the part of the component I consider most likely to determine its overall effectiveness.

## 3. METHODOLOGY

This component follows a quantitative, experimental research methodology. The research question whether adding calibrated uncertainty to a graph based knowledge tracing model changes what a downstream planner does is answered by measurable, numeric outcomes (AUC, ECE, Brier score), not by interpretation of qualitative data, and the underlying data (student interaction logs) is already structured and quantitative. This also matches how the baseline models I am comparing against (GKT, GIKT) were themselves evaluated in the literature, which makes a like for like experimental *(sentence ends here in the original document)*

### 3.1 Data / Participants / Experimental Inputs

I am using the ASSISTments benchmark rather than EdNet, and I want to be explicit about why, since this was a direct point raised in supervision. ASSISTments is the dataset GKT and GIKT were themselves evaluated on [1], [2], [7], so using it lets me compare my AUC figures against their published numbers directly rather than re-implementing their models on a different dataset and hoping the comparison still holds. It is also a more manageable size for building and debugging a concept-level prerequisite graph, which is central to this component. EdNet is considerably larger and was built for large scale deep learning rather than small, interpretable concept graphs, and since neither dataset actually contains Data Structures content specifically, I am using ASSISTments to validate the modelling method itself calibration and cold-start behavior, which should transfer across domains, while Component 1's graph supplies the actual Data Structures structure the full system will eventually run on [8].

From the raw interaction logs, I extract four fields per interaction: whether the answer was correct, response time, hint count, and the skill/concept tag. These are aggregated per student-concept pair into three features that become each graph node's input vector, recent rolling accuracy, attempt count, and time since the last attempt on that concept. Preparation involves cleaning and deduplicating records, mapping ASSISTments' skill identifiers onto concept nodes, and splitting the data chronologically per student, not randomly so that the model is never evaluated on a student's past using information from that same student's future.

### 3.2 Evaluation Metrics

Accuracy alone, including threshold-based measures such as AUC, cannot fully demonstrate the contribution of this component, particularly when the system is intended to provide both mastery estimates and associated uncertainty. Calibration is therefore an important aspect of the evaluation. For this reason, Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score are given equal weight to AUC in this evaluation rather than being treated as secondary metrics. This ensures that the evaluation considers not only predictive discrimination but also whether the model's confidence estimates are reliable.

| Metric | Why Relevant | Target / Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive Accuracy (AUC) | Standard knowledge-tracing metric; needed for a fair comparison against GKT/GIKT | Match or exceed published GKT / GIKT AUC on ASSISTments |
| Calibration (ECE) | Measures whether a stated confidence matches real reliability - the core contribution of this component | Lower ECE than an uncalibrated (single score) baseline |
| Calibration (Brier Score) | A complementary calibration measure that penalizes confident but wrong predictions more heavily | Lower Brier score than an uncalibrated baseline |
| Cold-Start Quality (ablation) | Isolates whether prerequisite graph priors actually help learners with little history | Improved AUC/ECE for low history learners with priors enabled vs. disabled |
| Downstream Utility | Confirms the uncertainty signal is not just reported, but changes what a consuming planner would do | Uncertainty reliably flags low confidence concepts for verification |

*Table 3 – Evaluation Metrics for Component 2*

### 3.3 Validation Strategy

Results are validated through a held out chronological test split (never seen during training), direct comparison against the published GKT/GIKT figures on the same benchmark, and a dedicated ablation study removing the cold-start priors to confirm they are actually responsible for any improvement observed, rather than the improvement coming from the GNN architecture alone.

### 3.4 Development Approach

I am using an iterative, experiment driven development approach rather than a fixed Scrum structure, since the actual unit of progress here is a completed experiment (train, evaluate, compare) rather than a shippable feature. Each iteration fixes one variable - first the GNN architecture, then the Monte Carlo Dropout sampling count, then the cold-start prior and measures its effect before moving to the next, which keeps any improvement traceable to a single cause.

### 3.5 Justifying the Monte Carlo Dropout Sampling Count

This subsection exists directly in response to a question raised in supervision - whether 30 forward passes is a 'default' value for Monte Carlo Dropout. Having checked this against the literature, it is not Gal and Ghahramani's original work and subsequent applications use sample counts ranging from around 10 to over 100 depending on the task and how much inference latency is acceptable [4]. There is no single agreed default. I am treating this as a hyperparameter to be justified empirically. I will run the same trained model at T ∈ {10, 20, 30, 50, 100} stochastic passes, and report how ECE, Brier score, and inference time change with T. The value I ultimately select for the final system will be the smallest T beyond which calibration quality stops improving materially, rather than a number I adopted because it appeared most often in other papers

Component 2 has no direct end user interface, it is an internal modelling service consumed by Component 3, not something a learner interacts with directly. Its requirements were gathered through stakeholder discussion with my three teammates, as the direct downstream users of this component's output, rather than through learner-facing interviews or surveys, which are more appropriate to Component 4's user study.
