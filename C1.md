## ABSTRACT

Online learning platforms face a trade-off that neither dominant approach fully resolves. Rule-based adaptive systems enforce prerequisites reliably but cannot personalize to an individual learner, while large language model (LLM) tutors personalize fluently but cannot be fully trusted, since they may skip prerequisites or hallucinate facts with no way to verify the output before it reaches a learner. Within the four-component GenAir system that addresses this gap, my individual contribution, Component 1, builds the shared structure on which the other three components depend: a pedagogy-typed knowledge graph.

Existing educational knowledge graphs, such as EDUKG and K12EduKG, extract only generic "A relates to B" relations, while prior prerequisite-inference work captures a single relation type with no verification step and no gold-standard evaluation. This is my identified research gap: no existing pipeline extracts teaching specific relation types, verify them symbolically, and prove their accuracy against a hand-annotated gold standard, all within one construction process.

I propose to close this gap with a hybrid LLM-plus-symbolic-verifier pipeline that reads raw Data Structures and Algorithms (DSA) lecture transcripts sourced from two publicly available MOOC caption datasets the Princeton Algorithms course (59 transcripts) and the UC San Diego Data Structures and Algorithms course (263 transcripts) and produces a graph carrying four pedagogy-typed edges: prerequisite-of, cognitive-load, bloom-level, and common-misconception-of. These relation types are absent from standard ontologies such as ACM CCS and IEEE LOM, meaning that no prior system can enforce cognitive difficulty, thinking-level appropriateness, or misconception clearing during path planning or explanation generation.

An LLM proposes candidate concepts and relations, while a rule-based symbolic verifier rejects edges that violate structural constraints, such as cycles, self-loops, an unapproved misconception pairing, or a Bloom-level inconsistency, before anything is stored in a Neo4j graph database. The development follows an iterative pipeline approach: raw transcripts are first cleaned and chunked, concept nodes are extracted and deduplicated using GPT-4o, typed relations are extracted through structured prompting, cognitive load is computed deterministically from prerequisite depth, and the symbolic verifier is applied as a final gate before graph storage.

The research follows a quantitative, corpus-based methodology. Extraction quality is measured using per-relation-type Precision, Recall, and F1 against a 200-300-sentence hand-annotated gold corpus, with inter-annotator agreement reported using Cohen's Kappa. Following panel feedback, this evaluation is extended beyond aggregate per-edge-type scores to a direct, subgraph-level correctness check: sampled local neighborhoods of the extracted graph are compared node-for-node and edge-for-edge against the same region of the gold corpus, so that errors concentrated in one part of the graph cannot be hidden behind a good average score.

Within the complete GenAir solution, Component 1 has no upstream dependency and is the first component to run. Its verified graph serves as the shared ground truth that Component 2's learner model, Component 3's path planner, and Component 4's explanation verifier each read from, making a trustworthy knowledge graph the precondition for every other guarantee the system makes.

**Keywords:** knowledge graph construction, pedagogy-typed relations, large language models, symbolic verification, gold-corpus evaluation

## 1 INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Background and Context

Every online learning platform today faces the same fundamental trade-off. Rule-based adaptive systems enforce prerequisites correctly but give every student the same fixed path, with limited ability to personalise or explain content in natural language. LLM-based tutors take the opposite approach: they personalise fluently but may hallucinate facts, skip prerequisites, and provide no reliable way to verify their output before it reaches a learner, as identified in recent surveys of LLM-based educational agents [Chu et al., 2025]-[9] and knowledge-graph-based learning path systems [Electronics, 2026]-[10]. Neither approach alone is sufficiently trustworthy for deployment at scale.

This problem directly affects two groups. Learners outside well-resourced institutions, who may rely on AI tutoring as a substitute for human guidance, bear the cost of an unverified system by being pushed into material for which they are not prepared or by wasting time recovering content they already understand. Instructors and institutions, meanwhile, lack appropriate tools to determine whether an AI-generated learning path is pedagogically sound before it is presented to a student.

The scale of this problem is significant. The global e-learning market is projected to reach USD 400 billion by 2026, with a large share specifically involving adaptive and AI-personalised platforms [9]. In Sri Lanka, the shift towards online and blended learning accelerated following COVID-19, with institutions such as SLIIT adopting LMS platforms for core course delivery. This makes the reliability of AI-generated educational content a locally relevant concern rather than a distant one.

Existing solutions address different parts of this problem in isolation. Knowledge graph construction systems such as EDUKG and K12EduKG extract only generic "A relates to B" links, without teaching-specific meaning or a verification step. Prerequisite-inference approaches capture only a single relation type and lack gold-standard evaluation. Commercial LLM tutors such as Khanmigo personalise fluently but are closed systems, meaning that their internal structure, if any exists, cannot be inspected or externally audited.

The GenAir system addresses this gap through four tightly coupled components that share a single verified knowledge graph. Component 1 builds a pedagogy-typed knowledge graph, providing a verified map of the subject domain. Component 2 uses this graph to model each learner's mastery and uncertainty using a GNN. Component 3 generates and verifies a personalised study path through a proposer-critic loop. Component 4 generates natural-language explanations and checks each claim for factual correctness and cognitive appropriateness.

My individual contribution is Component 1, which serves as the shared backbone on which every other component depends. Before the system can model a learner, plan a learning path, or verify an explanation, it requires a map of the subject domain that identifies which concepts require others, how demanding each concept is, what level of thinking it requires, and where learners typically make mistakes. Component 1 is responsible for constructing this map.

This research aligns with United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 4 (Quality Education), specifically Target 4.4, by making personalised, prerequisite-correct learning guidance verifiable and scalable rather than dependent on individual instructor effort or unverified AI output. Within SLIIT, the project falls under the Centre of Excellence for AI (CoEAI) research cluster in the EdTech and e-learning vertical. Component 1 specifically supports this goal by making the subject-domain map underlying a personalised learning system openly constructable, verifiable, and measurable, thereby reducing dependency on proprietary closed systems that cannot be externally audited.

### 1.2 Literature Review

#### 1.2.1 In General

This review focuses on two bodies of work that Component 1 sits directly between: educational knowledge graph construction methods that extract concepts and relations from text, and the small set of prerequisite-inference and ontology work that has tried to capture teaching-specific structure without a verification or evaluation step.

#### 1.2.2 Educational Knowledge Graph Construction: EDUKG and K12EduKG

EDUKG builds a large-scale, heterogeneous knowledge graph for K-12 education, with an ontology of 635 categories and 445 relation types and roughly 252 million extracted entities [9]. EDUKG was constructed from Chinese K-12 school textbooks across subjects including mathematics, science, and history, using a combination of named-entity recognition and relation extraction pipelines running over structured and semi-structured text.

Its primary strength is demonstrating that automated, large-scale graph construction from educational text is not only feasible but scalable to hundreds of millions of entities a proof of concept that directly motivates the automated approach taken in Component 1. It demonstrates that large-scale automated extraction from textbooks is feasible, but its relation types remain generic topic-to-topic association rather than teaching-specific structure and extracted edges are stored without a rule-based verification pass, so incorrect or weak edges are not filtered out. K12EduKG similarly automates knowledge graph construction from K-12 material but does not label the pedagogical type of a relation, has no verification layer, and is not connected to any downstream student-modelling or path-planning system. K12EduKG was constructed from English-language K-12 curriculum materials, with its primary strength being a fully automated extraction pipeline requiring no manual annotation at construction time.

However, like EDUKG, it reports only overall graph scale rather than per-relation-type accuracy, making it impossible to assess how reliable any individual extracted link actually is. Both papers report graph scale, not per-relation-type extraction accuracy, so it is not possible to know from either paper how reliable any single relation type actually is.

These findings directly influence Component 1's design in two ways: the feasibility of automated extraction from educational text justifies using an LLM-based extraction pipeline, while the absence of a verification step and per-type evaluation in both papers motivates the symbolic verifier and gold-corpus evaluation that form the core of this component's contribution.

#### 1.2.3 Prerequisite Relation Inference: Multi-Criteria Approaches

A separate line of work, represented by papers such as 'Inferring Prerequisite Knowledge Concepts in Educational Knowledge Graphs: A Multi-Criteria Approach' [citation], focuses narrowly on inferring prerequisite relations between concepts using unsupervised, multi-criteria signals such as co-occurrence frequency and the order concepts appear in course material. This approach needs no labelled training data, but it only ever produces one relation type prerequisite with no cognitive-load, Bloom-level, or misconception information, and because it is unsupervised, there is no gold-standard comparison to establish how accurate the inferred prerequisites are. Errors produced during inference are also never filtered by any subsequent symbolic check.

This finding influences Component 1 by confirming that prerequisite inference from raw text is tractable, while the absence of multi-type extraction and symbolic verification in prior work identifies exactly the gap this component addresses.

#### 1.2.4 Standard Educational Ontologies: ACM CCS and IEEE LOM

The ACM Computing Classification System (ACM CCS) and IEEE Learning Object Metadata (IEEE LOM) are the closest existing standards for describing educational content structure. Both provide vocabulary for classifying topics and metadata about learning objects, but neither defines a first-class relation type for prerequisite strength, cognitive load, Bloom-level thinking demand, or common misconceptions between concepts the four relation types this component introduces do not exist as first-class edges in either standard.

This confirms that the four edge types proposed in Component 1 prerequisite-of, cognitive-load, bloom-level, and common-misconception-of do not already exist in any standard that a downstream system could simply adopt, justifying their introduction as a novel schema contribution.

#### 1.2.5 Verification and Evaluation Practice in Knowledge Graph Construction

Across the papers reviewed above, a consistent pattern emerges: whichever extraction method is used large-scale automated pipelines, unsupervised inference, or manual ontology curation none pairs the extraction step with both a symbolic, rule-based verification pass and a hand-annotated gold-standard evaluation reported per relation type. This gap in evaluation practice is precisely what a panel reviewer would expect a defensible construction pipeline to close, and it is the direct motivation for the two-stage verification-and-evaluation design in Section 4 of this proposal.

#### 1.2.6 Existing Products and Systems

Two categories of deployed products attempt to solve parts of this problem. Khanmigo, developed by Khan Academy, is a conversational AI tutor built on GPT-4 that personalises explanations and hints for individual learners across K-12 subjects. It is used by millions of learners on the Khan Academy platform. Its primary function is adaptive dialogue; it responds to a student's specific question rather than delivering a fixed lecture. However, Khanmigo does not publish the structure of any underlying concept map, does not report prerequisite-violation rates, and provides no external verification methodology for its outputs. As a closed commercial system, its internal graph if one exists cannot be inspected, verified, or compared against academically. This means its personalisation is visible to users, but the structure and correctness of what drives it are not.

Traditional LMS platforms such as Moodle and Canvas support prerequisite gating a student cannot access Module B until Module A is completed but this is implemented as a flat, manually configured rule with no semantic understanding of why A must precede B, no cognitive load or Bloom-level information, and no natural-language explanation capability. These systems serve tens of millions of learners globally but require instructors to manually maintain the prerequisite structure, which does not scale and cannot adapt to individual learner states.

The limitation that Component 1 directly addresses across both categories is the same: neither closed LLM tutors nor rule-based LMS platforms expose a verified, pedagogy-typed knowledge graph that an external system can query, audit, and build upon.

### 1.3 Research Gap

Large-scale knowledge graph construction (EDUKG, K12EduKG) demonstrates that automated extraction from educational text is feasible, and separately, prerequisite-inference work demonstrates that a single teaching-relevant relation type can be recovered from text but no published pipeline extracts multiple pedagogy-typed relations, verifies them symbolically, and proves their accuracy against a hand-annotated gold standard in one construction process. The specific gap this component investigates is: existing educational knowledge graphs tell a downstream system that two concepts are related, but never how they are related in a teaching sense, nor how much that extracted relation should be trusted.

## 3 METHODOLOGIES

This component follows a quantitative, corpus-based research methodology. The research question whether a hybrid LLM-plus-symbolic-verifier pipeline can extract pedagogy-typed relations at a measurable, defensible accuracy is answered by numeric outcomes (Precision, Recall, F1, Cohen's Kappa) computed against a hand-annotated reference, not by interpretation of qualitative data.

### 3.1 Which research methodology or research design is appropriate

A quantitative, corpus-based research methodology is selected for three reasons. First, the research question of whether a hybrid LLM-plus-symbolic-verifier pipeline can extract pedagogy-typed relations at a measurable accuracy produces a numeric answer by definition: Precision, Recall, and F1 are quantities, not interpretations, and the research question is only answerable by computing them. Second, the data is text, making corpus-based methodology the standard and appropriate choice in NLP and information-extraction research, consistent with how comparable extraction pipelines such as EDUKG and prerequisite-inference work are evaluated. Third, the research does not involve human participants or subjective experience, ruling out qualitative or mixed methods designs. Quantitative evaluation against a hand-annotated gold standard is the accepted practice in relation-extraction research and is what a panel reviewer would expect to see as the primary evaluation design for this type of pipeline.

### 3.2 Data / Participants / Experimental Inputs

The corpus consists of two publicly available MOOC caption datasets: the Princeton Algorithms course (59 lecture transcripts, taught by Robert Sedgewick, covering 13 DSA topic categories) and the UC San Diego Data Structures and Algorithms course (263 lecture transcripts, covering 28 DSA topic categories). Each record contains the raw transcript text, part-of-speech tagged tokens, topic category, video ID, and course ID. Neither dataset contains pre-labelled concepts or relations this is expected and intentional, since extracting and typing those relations automatically is the research problem this component solves. Raw text is cleaned of transcription noise and split into overlapping word-based chunks before extraction, so that a relation spanning a chunk boundary is not missed.

Both datasets are selected for three reasons. First, they are publicly available for academic research use with no access restrictions. Second, they cover the DSA domain across 41 total topic categories sufficient breadth to produce a meaningful concept graph with varied relation types. Third, they provide raw lecture text rather than structured notes, which makes the extraction task more realistic and demonstrates the pipeline's ability to handle natural spoken-language content.

Both datasets were obtained from publicly released MOOC caption repositories. The Princeton dataset was accessed via the course caption release associated with the Coursera Algorithms Part I and II courses. The UC San Diego dataset was accessed from the same publicly available MOOC caption collection. No web scraping or special access was required both are distributed as JSON files for research use.

### 3.3 Baseline and Benchmark

The proposed pipeline is compared against two baselines to demonstrate the value of each of its two novel elements.

**Baseline 1 - LLM-only extraction (no symbolic verifier):**
The same LLM extraction pass is run without applying the symbolic verifier afterward. Precision, Recall, and F1 per relation type are computed for this unverified output and compared against the verified pipeline's scores. This ablation directly measures the precision lift contributed by the symbolic verifier if the verifier adds no value, the two results will be similar; if it filters genuine errors, the verified pipeline will show higher Precision.

**Baseline 2 - Unsupervised prerequisite inference (prior work):**
The multi-criteria prerequisite inference approach from prior literature, which extracts only prerequisite edges using co-occurrence and ordering signals, is used as a comparison point for the prerequisite-of edge type only. This establishes that the LLM-based extraction in Component 1 is at least competitive with the existing best method for that one relation type, while also producing three additional relation types that the baseline cannot. Both comparisons are run on the same gold corpus so that the metric values are directly comparable.

### 3.5 Validation Strategy

Results are validated in three stages. First, the symbolic verifier itself is an automatic validation layer: every proposed edge either passes all four structural rules or is rejected and logged with a reason, before it ever reaches the graph. Second, a 200–300 sentence gold corpus is built by two team members annotating independently, with Cohen's Kappa computed to confirm the annotation is reliable before it is used as ground truth; the system's extracted relations are then compared against this gold standard to compute Precision, Recall, and F1 separately per relation type.

Third, and directly in response to panel feedback, aggregate per-relation-type scores are not treated as sufficient on their own, since a good average score can still hide errors that are concentrated in one part of the graph rather than spread evenly across it for example, every misconception edge in one lecture topic being wrong while every other topic is extracted correctly would still produce an acceptable overall F1. To catch this, a sample of complete local neighbourhoods is drawn from the extracted graph a target concept together with all of its direct prerequisite, cognitive-load, and misconception edges and each sampled neighbourhood is checked node-for-node and edge-for-edge against the same neighbourhood as annotated in the gold corpus. A neighbourhood counts as correct only if every node and every edge in it matches; this whole-neighbourhood correctness rate is reported alongside, not instead of, the per-edge-type Precision, Recall, and F1 figures, since the two together show both how the pipeline performs on average and whether that average is hiding pockets of concentrated error.

### 3.6 Development Approach

I am using an iterative, pipeline-stage development approach: each stage of the pipeline concept extraction, relation extraction, symbolic verification, cognitive-load computation, Neo4j storage is built and checked on a small sample before being run over the full corpus, so that an error is traceable to the stage that introduced it rather than surfacing only once the whole pipeline is combined. The gold corpus and its evaluation are built last, once the pipeline's output is stable, so the annotation guidelines can be written against the actual relation types of the pipeline produces.

An iterative pipeline-stage approach is justified by the nature of the product: the pipeline has five sequential stages, each of which can introduce independent errors, and a waterfall or big-bang approach would make it impossible to isolate which stage caused a downstream problem. Iterative development where each stage is tested on a small sample before full-corpus execution is standard practice in NLP pipeline engineering and directly matches the sequential dependency structure of the component. Agile or Scrum would be inappropriate here since this is a solo research component with no sprint-team structure; the iteration cycle is a single researcher testing and refining each stage independently.

## 4 HIGH-LEVEL SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

### 4.1 Overview and Component Diagram

GenAir is a four-component middleware built around one shared, pedagogy-typed knowledge graph: Component 1 (mine) constructs the graph, Component 2 models the learner against it, Component 3 plans a verified learning path across it, and Component 4 generates explanations checked against it. Figure 1 shows the internal pipeline of my own component how raw lecture transcripts become a verified, evaluated knowledge graph.

> 2. **Concept Extraction Module** — Uses GPT-4o with POS tagging to identify and deduplicate concept nodes.

Component 1 consists of five internal sub-modules. The Text Preparation Module cleans and chunks raw transcripts into overlapping segments. The Concept Extraction Module uses GPT-4o with POS-tag guidance to identify and deduplicate concept nodes. The Relation Extraction Module uses a second GPT-4o pass to classify typed edges into the four-edge schema. The Symbolic Verifier Module applies four structural rules to reject invalid edges before storage. The Storage and Evaluation Module computes cognitive-load, stores the verified graph in Neo4j, and runs the gold-corpus evaluation pipeline.

### 4.2 Overall Integration

Component 1 has no upstream dependency it is the first component to run, since every other component reads from the graph it produces. Its output, the verified pedagogy-typed knowledge graph, is consumed by Component 2 as structural priors for cold-start learners, by Component 3's symbolic critic as the rule set a candidate study path is checked against, and by Component 4 as the source of Bloom-level labels used to check whether an explanation is pitched at the right level.

Integration between components is planned through a shared REST API with a JSON schema that Component 1 owns for the outgoing knowledge-graph payload, so that each downstream component can be developed and tested independently against a fixed contract. Specifically, Component 1 exposes two REST endpoints `/api/kg/build` which triggers full graph construction from a dataset, and `/api/kg/update` which adds new verified edges both returning a JSON payload following the shared team schema where each node carries its concept name, bloom-level, and normalised cognitive-load score, and each edge carries its type, weight, and source provenance.

## 5 USER REQUIREMENTS

Component 1 has no direct end-user interface it is an internal graph-construction service consumed by Components 2, 3, and 4, not something a learner interacts with directly. Its requirements were gathered through stakeholder discussions with my three teammates, as the direct downstream consumers of the graph, and through supervision meetings with my supervisor and co-supervisor. From these discussions, my teammates confirmed the exact query interface they need (typed edges retrievable per concept), and my supervisor's feedback specifically raised the requirement, reflected below, that extraction accuracy must be validated at the subgraph level, not only through aggregate per-edge-type scores.

Requirements were gathered through three structured activities. First, a stakeholder discussion session was held with all four team members to agree on the exact JSON payload schema and query interface that Components 2, 3, and 4 need from the graph this produced the interoperability requirement NFR4 and the REST API requirement FR4. Second, domain expert input was obtained through two supervision meetings with Prof. Nuwan Kodagoda and Mr. Jeewaka Perera, whose feedback on evaluation rigour produced the subgraph-level correctness requirement in FR5 and NFR2. Third, a brief review of how comparable knowledge graph construction papers report evaluation results specifically EDUKG and the prerequisite-inference literature confirmed that per-relation-type Precision, Recall, and F1 against a gold standard is the accepted standard in this domain, which shaped FR5 directly.

## 6 COMMERCIALIZATION PLAN

**Target Market and Customer Persona:** Universities, MOOC platforms such as Coursera and edX, and corporate learning-and-development teams. The primary customer persona is an EdTech product manager or LMS administrator evaluating whether to trust an AI-personalised learning path enough to deploy it on a scale.

**Value Proposition:** Personalised, prerequisite-respecting learning paths with verified explanations where every downstream decision is grounded in a knowledge graph whose accuracy has been measured, not assumed. Component 1 specifically provides this guarantee: the extraction pipeline produces a graph with known Precision, Recall, and F1 scores per relation type, which an institutional buyer can inspect and audit.

**Revenue Model**

- SaaS licensing - monthly API access fee charged to LMS platforms
- Usage-based pricing - per active learner per month
- Consulting - custom graph construction for new subject domains (e.g. building a pedagogy-typed graph for a new university course)

**Cost Estimation:** See Section 8 (Budget). Primary cost is LLM API usage estimated under USD 5 for the full corpus. Infrastructure costs are shared across the four-component team. No hardware purchase required.

**Pricing Strategy:** Tiered pricing: a free tier for small institutions (up to 500 learners) to drive adoption, a standard SaaS tier for mid-size platforms, and an enterprise tier for large MOOC platforms with custom SLA and dedicated graph-construction support.

**Competitive Advantage:** Unlike closed commercial tutors such as Khanmigo, GenAir's knowledge graph and its extraction accuracy are fully measurable and can be reported to an institutional buyer in a vendor evaluation. This auditability is a claim no current competitor can make, and Component 1's gold-corpus evaluation directly produces the accuracy evidence that supports it.

**Intellectual Property:** Research outputs will be published under an open-source licence. The graph schema and hybrid LLM-plus-symbolic-verifier pipeline are being considered for patent filing as a novel neuro-symbolic construction method. The gold corpus and evaluation suite will be retained by the research team and SLIIT.
